How to Keep a Travel Journal: Making Memories Last

2026-02-02 | Hoonjae Park · myTravel Travel Content Team

Have you ever noticed that after you return home, the experiences you once lived so vividly begin to blur as time passes? The taste of the sangria you drank in a Barcelona alley, the stillness you felt at dawn in a Kyoto bamboo grove, the good-humored haggling with a merchant at the Istanbul bazaar. These were unforgettable moments, and yet a few months later the specific feelings and details fade away, leaving only a vague memory that it was "nice." Photos show you the scene, but they can't capture how you felt at the time.

A travel journal is the most powerful way to solve this problem. A written record brings memories back to life in a way that's fundamentally different from photographs. Jot down just a few lines about what you smelled, who you talked to and about what, and how you felt in that moment, and years later, rereading it can transport you back to that instant as if you'd stepped into a time machine. In this article, we'll cover everything about capturing your travels — from the value of a travel journal, to what you should record, to the tools you can use, and practical ways to turn journaling into a lasting habit.

The Value of a Travel Journal: Why Record at All

The reason to keep a travel journal goes beyond simply "remembering." Recording is an act that fundamentally transforms the depth of your travel experience. According to psychological research, the process of organizing an experience into writing prompts the brain to process it more deeply and convert it into long-term memory. In other words, the very act of keeping a travel journal makes the trip itself richer.

Preserving memory is the most basic value of a travel journal. Human memory fades astonishingly fast. According to the forgetting curve identified by German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus, we forget roughly 70% of new information within 24 hours. Travel experiences are no different. The details of a moving moment you felt today will be more than half gone by tomorrow. Recording the day's experience that same night is an effective way to prevent this forgetting. Five or ten years later, you can still recall that moment as vividly as if it happened yesterday.

Capturing emotion is a role unique to a journal that photos can never fill. A photograph captures a visual scene, but it can't hold the emotions you felt looking at it, the thoughts running in the background, or the connection you shared with the person beside you. Feelings like "my throat tightened the moment I stepped into St. Peter's Basilica" or "my heart pounded as I watched the train pass on the Hanoi railway" can only be preserved in writing. When you read these emotional records later, they stir a deep sense of empathy and remembrance that goes far beyond mere information.

There's also great value as a reference for future trips. Honest notes about which accommodations were good, which restaurants offered the best value, and which attractions fell short become a precious reference when planning your next journey. And when you revisit the same city, rereading your earlier records makes it easy to decide what new places to see and where you'd like to return. The way travel bloggers and influencers turn their trip logs into content is a good illustration of the reference value of a good record.

What to Record: The 7 Key Elements of a Travel Journal

The most common worry when facing a travel journal is "What should I even write?" Feeling stuck in front of a blank page is completely natural. But knowing in advance what elements to record lowers that barrier dramatically. Keep the following seven key elements in mind.

1. Places and Your Route

This is the most basic record. Write down where you went today and in what order you moved. Even simply mapping out your route — "left the hotel → subway to Shibuya Station → walked to Meiji Shrine → Takeshita Street in Harajuku → a cafe in Omotesando" — helps give your day structure. Noting the rough amount of time spent at each place is also a great help when planning the itinerary for your next trip.

2. Food and Flavors

Food is an essential experience that no trip can do without. Record which restaurant you ate at, what you had, how much it cost, and how it tasted. "Pad thai from a street stall on Yaowarat Road in Bangkok, 50 baht. The bean sprouts were crisp and the peanuts nutty. A squeeze of lime turned it into a completely different dish." Writing in this kind of specific detail brings the flavor back the moment you reread it. Recording it alongside a photo of the food is even better.

3. People You Met and Conversations

The exchanges with people you meet while traveling often become the most memorable moments. Write down who you talked with and about what — a fellow traveler at your lodging, a local guide, a market vendor, a cafe barista. Asking for and noting their name makes it even more special. "Markus, a German I met at the hostel. He said he'd quit his IT job and was traveling around the world. He mentioned he wanted to visit Jeju Island in Korea, so I recommended some spots." These records of encounters capture the human side of travel.

4. Feelings and Thoughts

This is the key element that sets a travel journal apart from an ordinary guidebook or review. Write honestly about the emotions you felt in a particular place, the thoughts that came to you during the trip, and any fresh realizations about yourself. "Watching the sunrise over Angkor Wat, I thought the people who built this must have seen the same sky. Centuries of time felt collapsed into a single instant." Personal emotional records like this land the most powerfully, even years later.

Recording tip: If you find it hard to put emotions into words, try leaning on your five senses. Recall and jot down, one by one, what you saw, what you heard, what you smelled, what you touched, and what you tasted in that moment — and the atmosphere of that instant will come through vividly and naturally.

5. Episodes and Mishaps

Things that didn't go as planned, unexpected discoveries, funny mistakes — the small and large episodes of a trip are the parts that read most enjoyably later on. "I followed Google Maps but ended up somewhere completely wrong. And yet that's exactly where I stumbled onto a hidden bakery only the locals knew about. Best croissant of my life." These chance episodes are the real fun of travel. Remember that unexpected moments make better stories than a flawless trip.

6. Weather and Atmosphere

Weather has a big impact on the travel experience, yet it's one of the first things forgotten if you don't record it. "A light drizzle fell from the morning. Walking under an umbrella, the cobblestones of Paris glistened. Paris in the rain had a charm all its own." Recording the weather together with the mood it created makes the day's scene come alive in three dimensions. The seasonal feel of a destination, the temperature gap between morning and evening, humidity, and so on also become practical reference points for planning your next trip.

7. Recording Expenses

Noting how much you spent and on what is also an important part of a travel journal. Jotting down accommodation, transportation, meals, admission fees, and shopping costs lets you grasp your total spending after the trip, and it's a big help for budgeting your next journey too. "Total spend today: transport €15, lunch €22, museum admission €14, cafe €6 = €57 total." When this simple recording habit accumulates, it builds up data on your average daily costs by country and by city.

Comparing Recording Tools: Finding the One That Fits You

There are many tools for keeping a travel journal. From the traditional notebook to digital apps and social media, each has its own strengths and weaknesses, and choosing a tool that suits your temperament is the key to recording consistently.

Paper Notebook: The Charm of Analog

The paper notebook is the most traditional and romantic tool for a travel journal. The act of writing by hand with a pen invites a different depth of thought than digital typing. Research shows that writing by hand activates more regions of the brain and aids memory retention. Being able to paste in postcards, tickets, and receipts you picked up along the way and decorate it like a scrapbook is another appeal. It needs no charging, you can use it freely even during takeoff and landing, and you can write anytime, anywhere without an internet connection. The downsides are the risk of losing it, the difficulty of backing it up, and how inconvenient it is to search the contents later.

Digital Note Apps: Convenience and Efficiency

Digital note apps like Evernote, Notion, Google Keep, and Apple Notes have convenience as their biggest advantage. You can jot things down quickly on your smartphone anytime, and attach photos and voice memos alongside your text. Cloud syncing means no worry about losing your records, and tags and search make it easy to find past entries. With Notion, you can create a template and record in the same format every day for a consistent travel journal. The downsides are dependence on battery and internet, and the fact that opening your phone can scatter your focus with notifications from other apps.

Social Media: The Joy of Sharing in Real Time

There's also the approach of leaving your travel records on social media like Instagram, a blog, or Twitter. Sharing your travel experiences in real time and getting reactions from friends and family can be motivating. Location tags and hashtags also make it easy to organize things later. But by the nature of social media, posts can easily become about presenting a polished image for others, and they tend to stay surface-level rather than capturing honest emotion. Keeping a personal journal alongside your social sharing is the most ideal approach.

myTravel: Integrating Travel Planning and Journaling

The myTravel app is a tool that lets you manage both trip planning and journaling on a single platform. You can add each day's experiences, impressions, and photos to an AI-generated itinerary, so your plan and your record connect naturally. When you adjust your itinerary or add a new place during the trip, that very history of changes becomes a travel story of its own. After the trip, you can also share your completed travel log with other travelers through social features. This creates a virtuous cycle where you plan your own trips by referring to other people's travel records.

Recommended combination: During the trip, record briefly in the myTravel app in line with your itinerary, and after you return to your lodging in the evening, write out the day's feelings and episodes in detail in a paper notebook. That way you enjoy both the convenience of digital and the depth of analog at once.

Making Time to Journal: Building a Routine

"I don't have time to record while traveling" is the most common excuse — and a genuine difficulty. After sightseeing all day and coming back exhausted, you may have no energy left to write. But building a habitual routine solves this problem naturally.

Morning Routine: Recording the Day Before

Set your alarm 15 to 20 minutes earlier, and record the previous day's experiences over a cup of coffee. Organizing what you were too tired to write at night in the morning, with a clear head, lets you write more systematically. After a night's sleep, the core moments that truly stuck with you also get naturally filtered out. Arriving early at your lodging's breakfast time and recording quietly is another good approach.

Evening Routine: Wrapping Up the Day

This is the method of investing 15 to 20 minutes in recording before you fall asleep after returning to your lodging. Writing while the day's experiences are still fresh means you won't miss the details. Lying in bed, jotting down even just keywords in your phone's notes app is enough. It doesn't have to be perfect sentences. Even a string of keywords like "market, grilled fish, smell of spices, three cats, grandmother's smile" is plenty to reconstruct the scene later.

Recording in Transit: Using Spare Moments

Travel time on buses, trains, planes, and taxis is the ideal time for recording. On long journeys in particular, you have nothing else to do, so writing comes naturally. Using your smartphone's voice memo feature lets you leave your thoughts by speaking even when typing is hard. Later, you can listen back to the voice memo and transcribe it into text.

Recording With Photos: The Synergy of Image and Text

Recording photos and words together complements the limits of each and completes a far richer travel record. A photo captures the visual detail of a scene, while writing holds that scene's context and emotion.

Adding notes to photos is the most effective method. From the photos you took over the day, pick the 5 to 10 most memorable ones and attach a short caption to each. "It started pouring as I walked down this alley. The owner of the shop next door lent me an umbrella." Just one or two lines like this turns a photo from a plain image into a story. You can add descriptions in your phone's gallery app, or use the photo gallery feature in the myTravel app to connect photos and text naturally.

Making a photo journal is another wonderful method. After your trip, use a printing service to print out your key photos, paste them into a paper journal, and leave notes in the margins for a one-of-a-kind travel album. In the digital age, this kind of analog work takes on a special meaning of its own. Choosing, arranging, and captioning the photos yourself lets you savor the trip one more time.

A video journal is another approach that's become popular lately. You film a short 30-second to 1-minute video each day, then edit them after the trip into a single travel film. Because it captures sound and movement, it makes for an even more vivid record than photos. That said, editing takes a lot of time, so we recommend filming during the trip but leaving the editing for afterward, when you have some breathing room.

Wrapping Up After the Trip: Completing Your Record

Most of the records you make during a trip are hastily written notes, keywords, and short phrases. Organizing them after the trip turns them into a polished travel log. It's best to do this within a week or two of returning, because if too much time passes, your memory blurs and it becomes hard to make sense of the context of your notes.

Chronological organization is the most basic method. Sort your records by travel date and arrange your photos in time order to grasp the flow of the trip at a glance. Giving each date a title makes it even more structured. "Day 3: Kyoto's Thousand Torii — Fushimi Inari Shrine." Use the day's highlight as the title, like this.

Thematic organization is a fun approach too. Reorganizing your records by theme — food, people, scenery, cultural experiences — turns a simple diary into a travel essay. Arranging your writing around themes like "10 Bowls of Ramen I Ate in Tokyo," "People I Met on European Trains," or "Morning Scenes at Southeast Asian Temples" makes for more enjoyable reading, and it's well suited to sharing on a blog or social media.

Don't skip organizing lessons and recommendations either. Summarizing things like "what I'll definitely do if I go again," "what I'd recommend to the next traveler," and "mistakes or regrets" builds up travel know-how that's all your own. Tips grounded in this kind of real, hands-on experience are more valuable than anything in a guidebook.

The Joy of Sharing Your Travel Records

Sharing your own travel records with others multiplies their value. It's rewarding to know your experience helps someone, and inspires another person's journey.

Using myTravel's social features lets you share your travel plans and records naturally. When you make a completed trip public, other users can plan their own travels by referring to it, and connect with you through likes and comments. The fact that another traveler can follow the exact route you walked, the places you visited, and the food you ate creates a new kind of value for a travel record. Reading other travelers' records of the same destination can also help you discover places or experiences you missed.

Sharing with family and friends is another important joy of a travel record. When you share your organized records after returning from a trip, even people who couldn't come along can experience the journey vicariously. Telling your travel stories to parents or elderly relatives, in particular, can be a great gift. Made into a paper notebook or a photo book, it also makes a wonderful physical present.

Sharing tip: When you share your travel records, try including not only the positive experiences but the mistakes and failures too. A story like "I took a wrong turn and wandered for two hours, but thanks to that I found a hidden lookout point" gives the reader both empathy and enjoyment. An honest travel log earns more resonance than a flawless one.

The Recording Habits of Famous Travel Writers

How did the masters of travel writing keep their records? There's plenty we can learn from the habits of famous travel writers.

Paul Theroux is known for enjoying train travel and writing while in transit. In his work "The Great Railway Bazaar," he recorded in fine detail his conversations with the people he met on the trains. His habit was to turn travel time into writing time. We too can follow this habit just by jotting quick notes on our smartphones while on the move.

Bill Bryson is a travel writer famous for his humor and powers of observation. He has an eye for finding amusement in ordinary scenes, which comes from a habit of consciously observing and asking, "What's funny here?" Try practicing paying attention to the small things — the odd furniture arrangement in a hotel room, an amusing sight on the subway.

Haruki Murakami, the Japanese novelist and travel essayist, left behind travel writings such as "A Distant Drum" and "What on Earth Is in Laos?" He's known for keeping a strict routine of writing at a set time every day. He maintained this routine even while traveling, reportedly starting his day only after writing a set amount in the morning. It's a good example of how important a regular recording habit can be.

Korean travel writer Son Mina, a former broadcaster turned travel author, has a striking feature in her books: a way of recording that puts encounters with locals at the center. Rather than tourist attractions, her writing is rich with people-centered stories — the vendors at a neighborhood market, the owner of a guesthouse, a passenger on the same bus. She shows well that taking an interest in people and striking up conversations is the secret to a rich travel record.

In Closing: Start Your Travel Record Today

Keeping a travel journal doesn't require grand preparation. You don't need an expensive notebook or a special app. Just three lines in your phone's notes app is enough. "The single most memorable scene of today," "the most delicious thing I ate today," "one feeling I had today." Write just these three every day, and by the time the trip ends you'll have completed a record that's surprisingly rich.

What matters isn't writing perfectly, but recording consistently. It's fine if the spelling is off, the sentences are awkward, or you never quite finish. A trip left unrecorded fades in color as time passes, but a recorded trip can always be brought back into sharp focus. On your next trip, plan your itinerary with myTravel and start the habit of recording a little each day. It will be one of the best gifts you can give your future self.
