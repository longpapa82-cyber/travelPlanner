Solo Female Travel Safety: A Practical Guide to Lodging, Getting Around, and Nights Out

2026-07-10 | Hoonjae Park · myTravel Travel Content Team

Safety Starts Before You Book Anything

Solo travel as a woman is not about fear — it is about preparation. The travelers who move through unfamiliar cities with confidence are usually the ones who did quiet, boring homework before they left. Most uncomfortable situations are avoidable with a few decisions made in advance.

Start by narrowing your research to the neighborhood level, not just the city. A destination can have a glowing reputation overall while still having a handful of areas you want to avoid after dark. Read recent forum threads and traveler reports from the last 12 months — safety conditions shift, and a five-year-old blog post is not reliable.

Build a simple information sheet before departure that includes:

  • The local emergency number — it is not 911 everywhere (112 across the EU, 999 in the UK, 119 in parts of Asia).
  • The address and phone number of your country's nearest embassy or consulate.
  • Your accommodation's full address written in the local language, in case you need to show a driver.
  • A rough budget so you are never caught fumbling with cash in an unfamiliar area.

Having your booked address and each day's plan on one screen means you are not screenshotting six different apps when you need an address fast.

Choosing Accommodation That Actually Feels Safe

Where you sleep sets the tone for your whole trip. A great location can turn a nervous first night into an easy one. When comparing options, weigh safety factors as heavily as price.

What to look for

  • 24-hour reception or staffed entry. Late arrivals happen — flights get delayed. A property with someone at the desk around the clock is worth the small premium.
  • A central, well-lit location. Prioritize being within a short, walkable distance of transit and busy streets over a cheaper place on a quiet edge of town.
  • Recent reviews from other solo women. Filter reviews and search terms like "solo," "safe," and "female" to see how the neighborhood actually feels at night.
  • Female-only dorms if you are staying in hostels. Many now offer them, and they are one of the best value safety upgrades available.

Once you arrive

Do a quick check-in ritual every time you enter a new room. Confirm the door locks properly, locate the fire exit, and note whether windows facing walkways can be secured. A lightweight portable door lock or door-stop alarm weighs almost nothing and gives real peace of mind in rooms where the lock feels flimsy.

When you check in, avoid saying your room number out loud at a busy desk, and never announce that you are traveling alone to strangers. If someone asks, a vague "I'm meeting friends later" is a perfectly reasonable answer.

Getting Around Cities Without Stress

Transport is where many travelers feel most exposed, especially at night or when jet-lagged. A few habits make a huge difference.

Rideshares and taxis

  • Prefer app-based rideshares where you can see the driver's name, plate, and rating, and where your route is logged.
  • Confirm the plate and driver's name before getting in, and sit in the back seat.
  • Share your live trip with a friend or family member — most rideshare apps have a one-tap "share trip" button.
  • If a ride feels wrong, trust it. Ask to be let out somewhere busy and well-lit; you never owe anyone your continued presence.

Public transit and walking

Public transport is often the safest and cheapest way to move, but plan your routes in daylight so you are not deciphering a map on a dark platform. Keep your bag in front of you in crowds, and split your money — carry a small amount of cash in an accessible pocket and keep cards and the rest in a money belt or inner pocket.

When walking, project purpose. Move like you know where you are going even when you are checking directions. Step into a shop or café to look at your phone rather than stopping in the middle of a quiet street.

Plan routes before you go: Because myTravel lays out your itinerary by day with each stop's location, you can look at the shape of your day in advance and decide which legs to do by daylight and which to take a rideshare for. Planning the order of your stops so the later ones are closer to your accommodation is a small move that pays off after dark.

Nights Out: Enjoying Yourself and Staying in Control

You should not have to skip the evening scene to stay safe. You just want a few guardrails so you can relax.

  • Watch your drink. Order from the bar yourself, keep your glass in sight, and do not accept an already-poured drink from a stranger. If you lose sight of it, get a new one.
  • Set a cash and time limit before you head out. Deciding "home by 1, and this much cash" while sober is far easier than deciding it later.
  • Know how you are getting back. Pre-plan your route home before the first drink, not after the last one.
  • Keep your phone charged. Carry a small power bank. A dead phone at 1 a.m. in an unfamiliar area is the situation you most want to avoid.

Trust the people who work in the places you visit. Bartenders and hotel staff deal with uncomfortable situations constantly and are often your fastest allies if someone will not leave you alone. A quiet word to staff is completely normal and expected.

There is no shame in leaving early. The best solo travelers are the ones who listen to the small voice that says "time to go" — and go.

Staying Connected and Sharing Your Whereabouts

One of the biggest safety differences between solo and group travel is that no one automatically knows where you are. You have to build that in.

  • Share your itinerary with someone you trust. Give a friend or family member a rough outline of where you will be and when, and agree on a check-in rhythm — a quick message each evening is enough.
  • Turn on location sharing with one trusted person for the duration of your trip.
  • Get a reliable local connection. An eSIM or local SIM means you are never dependent on café Wi-Fi to call a ride or look up an address.
  • Save maps offline for your destination so you can navigate even without data.

A daily check-in is not about being watched — it is about having someone who would notice quickly if something were wrong. That single habit is one of the highest-value things a solo traveler can do.

When Something Goes Wrong: A Calm Plan

Most trips pass without incident, but the traveler who has thought through worst-case scenarios stays calmer if one arrives. Decide your responses in advance so you are not improvising under stress.

If you lose your passport or wallet

  1. Keep a photo of your passport and a scan of key documents in secure cloud storage and a printed copy in a separate bag.
  2. Note your embassy's location and hours before you travel — replacing a passport abroad is routine when you know where to go.
  3. Carry a backup payment method stored separately from your main wallet. If one is stolen, you are not stranded.

If you feel followed or unsafe

  • Head toward light, people, and open businesses — a hotel lobby, a pharmacy, a busy café.
  • Do not lead anyone to your accommodation. Change direction and confirm you are alone first.
  • Call your emergency contact and stay on the line while you move to safety.
Keep emergency info handy: Store your embassy address, local emergency number, and accommodation details right inside your myTravel trip notes so they travel with your plan. When you are stressed, one screen with the essentials beats searching your inbox for a booking email.

Preparation is what turns solo travel from something to be nervous about into one of the most rewarding things you can do. Do the homework, trust your instincts, and go.
