Long-Term Travel Prep Guide: Traveling for a Month or More

2026-01-30 | myTravel Team

A month, two months, or even longer. Long-term travel is an entirely different kind of experience from a short vacation. On a week-long trip you rush through the landmarks and tick off your itinerary, but on a long journey you can settle into a single city for days at a time and let its everyday life seep into you. Becoming a regular at a neighborhood cafe, getting to know the vendors at the market, gradually falling into the rhythm of a place — these are things a short trip can never give you.

At the same time, long-term travel demands far more preparation and planning. On a week-long trip you can pack loosely and get by with a single card, but for a journey of a month or more there is much more to think about: budgeting, health, accommodation strategy, minimizing luggage, staying in touch. This guide walks travelers who dream of a long trip through every stage — from the preparation before you leave, to managing life on the road, to easing back in once you return home.

The Appeal and the Challenges of Long-Term Travel

The greatest appeal of long-term travel is depth. If a week-long trip is a wide but shallow lake, a long journey is a narrow but deep well. Stay two or three weeks in a single city and you move beyond the tourist's gaze to actually "living" there. Knowing which bakery is the best, which park is the quietest, at what hour the sunset falls prettily on which alley — that is a reward only long-term travel can offer.

Economic efficiency is an unexpected upside of the long haul. You only buy the flight once, accommodation comes with long-stay discounts, and cooking for yourself can slash your food costs. Many long-term travelers testify that "a month-long trip costs far less per day than a week-long one." In Southeast Asia, a month's stay can even cost less than a month of ordinary living expenses back home.

But there are challenges, too. Travel fatigue is the problem long-term travelers face most often. For the first week or two everything feels fresh and exciting, but by the third week the constant moving, adjusting to new places, and language barriers can wear you down. Loneliness is another common feeling. Especially when traveling alone, there will inevitably come a time when you miss family and friends. Health issues also grow more likely the longer you travel. Knowing about these challenges in advance and preparing for them makes them far easier to overcome.

Budget Planning: Designing Your Long-Term Finances

Budgeting for a long trip needs to be far more systematic than for an ordinary one. It calls for a three-step approach: set a total budget, work out a daily budget, and secure an emergency fund.

Daily Budget Benchmarks by Country

Travel costs vary dramatically from country to country. Here are rough daily budgets by region for a thrifty backpacker.

  • Southeast Asia (Thailand, Vietnam, Cambodia, Indonesia): USD 25–40 a day. Hostel dorms under USD 8, a street-food meal USD 2–4, cheap transport.
  • Eastern Europe (Poland, Hungary, Czechia, Romania): USD 40–65 a day. Hostels around USD 15, a meal at a local restaurant around USD 8.
  • Western Europe (France, Germany, Italy, Spain): USD 65–120 a day. Hostels USD 25–40, meals USD 15–25.
  • South America (Bolivia, Peru, Ecuador): USD 25–50 a day. Colombia and Argentina run USD 35–65.
  • Japan: USD 55–100 a day. Capsule hotels USD 25–35, a meal at a convenience store or chain restaurant around USD 8.

These figures assume a thrifty travel style; if you want mid-range accommodation and meals, multiply by 1.5 to 2. Base your total travel budget on "daily budget × number of days," then add fixed costs such as flights, insurance, and visa fees on top.

Making the Most of Long-Stay Discounts

There are plenty of discounts only long-term travelers get to enjoy. Airbnb automatically applies weekly (7-plus nights) and monthly (28-plus nights) discounts, and the monthly discount can reach 20–50%. European rail passes (such as the Eurail pass) get cheaper per day the longer the pass lasts. Local gyms, coworking spaces, and language schools also offer discounts for long-term sign-ups. Airline mileage and hotel membership programs build up meaningful value over a long trip, too.

Budget tip: Set aside 10–15% of your total budget as an emergency fund. Unexpected costs — a sudden hospital visit, a flight change, a problem with your accommodation — are bound to come up. Travel on a tight budget with no cushion and even a small mishap can cause a lot of stress.

Minimalist Packing: A Month With a Single Bag

The most common mistake in long-term travel is bringing too much. About 90% of the things you pack "just in case" end up going unused. Experienced long-term travelers say it in unison: "Take half the luggage and twice the money."

One-bag travel is the ideal form of a long trip. You fit everything into a single 40–50-liter backpack or a carry-on-sized case. With less to carry, you move freely, save on excess baggage fees when flying budget airlines, and feel less burdened when changing accommodation. Managing your bag on buses and trains becomes far easier, too.

The key to minimizing clothing is laundry. Four or five tops, two or three bottoms, and five to seven pairs of underwear are plenty for a month or more. Choose quick-drying synthetic or merino-wool fabrics and they dry overnight after a hand wash. Most cities have coin laundromats, and in Southeast Asia laundry services cost as little as USD 1–2. If you keep all your clothes in a unified color palette (black, navy, gray, and so on), any combination looks natural.

Choosing multi-purpose items lets you cut down further. Bring a large scarf (a sarong) instead of a muffler and you can use it as a shawl, a blanket, a beach towel, a pillow cover, and more. Hiking trousers work as everyday wear, and some can zip off into shorts. Judge each item by whether it can serve several purposes.

Accommodation Strategy: The Right Options for a Long Stay

On a long trip your accommodation is not simply "a place to sleep" but "a space to live in." You therefore need to choose it by different criteria than on a short trip.

Monthly Airbnb

If you are staying two weeks or more in one city, a monthly Airbnb is usually the most sensible choice. You get a kitchen to cook in and a washing machine so laundry is no worry. With the monthly discount applied, it is highly competitive on price compared to hotels or hostels. Before you check in, ask the host about the Wi-Fi speed, the state of the kitchen equipment, and the location of nearby supermarkets. For a long stay, a comfortable living environment matters most.

Hostels and Guesthouses

For sociable travelers, hostels remain an appealing option. You can meet travelers from around the world easily, use a shared kitchen, and keep costs low. On a long stay you can also get friendly with the hostel staff and pick up local tips. Some hostels offer discounts for extended stays, or run "work exchange" programs that provide free lodging in return for helping around the property. That said, the lack of privacy and the noise of dorm life can become a real source of stress over a long stay, so consider a private room as well.

Workaway and House-Sitting

Workaway is a platform where you work four or five hours a day (farm work, helping run a hostel, language exchange, and so on) in exchange for free room and board. Not only does it eliminate your accommodation costs entirely, it also lets you take part deeply in the real lives of locals, making for a distinctive experience. There are opportunities of all kinds — farms in South America, ecovillages in Europe, guesthouses in Southeast Asia.

House-sitting (through TrustedHousesitters, Nomador, and the like) means staying in someone's home while they are away, caring for their pets or looking after the house. Lodging is completely free, and you get to live comfortably in a private home. It suits animal-loving travelers especially well. The trade-off is less flexibility, since you are tied to a specific place for a specific period.

Health Care: The Most Important Asset on a Long Trip

Health is what you need to watch most carefully on a long trip. On a short trip you can push through a little run-down feeling, but on a long journey a small health problem can throw off your entire itinerary.

Vaccinations

The vaccinations you need depend on your destination. Visit an international clinic or a travel-medicine specialist at least 4–6 weeks before departure for a consultation. The vaccines generally recommended for long-term travelers are as follows.

  • Hepatitis A: Essential for travel to Southeast Asia, South America, and Africa. Two doses give long-term immunity.
  • Hepatitis B: Recommended if you have not yet been vaccinated. Three doses.
  • Typhoid: Recommended for a long stay in Southeast Asia or South Asia.
  • Yellow fever: Required to enter some countries in Africa and South America. An International Certificate of Vaccination (the yellow card) is needed.
  • Japanese encephalitis: Recommended for a long stay in rural areas of Southeast Asia or South Asia.
  • Rabies: For places with many dogs and monkeys, such as India and Southeast Asia. Three doses.

First-Aid Kit and Medical Preparation

On a long trip you should pack a more complete first-aid kit than on an ordinary one. The basics are pain relievers, digestives, anti-diarrheal medication, antihistamines (for allergies), ointment (for wounds and insect bites), antiseptic, bandages, a thermometer, and oral rehydration salts (ORS). If you take any personal prescription medication, bring enough for the entire trip along with an English-language prescription. Some countries restrict the import of certain medications, so it is important to check in advance.

Be sure to get a dental check-up before you leave, too. Dental treatment abroad is very expensive and often hard to claim on insurance. Taking care of cavities, wisdom teeth, and gum problems beforehand reduces the chance of suffering from toothache on the road.

International Health Insurance

On a long trip, international health insurance is not optional — it is essential. Ordinary travel insurance usually covers only 30 or 90 days, so you need to look into insurance made for long-term travel. Global services such as World Nomads, SafetyWing, and IMG suit long-term travelers well. SafetyWing is popular because you can sign up on a monthly basis and adjust the coverage period flexibly to match your trip.

Things to check without fail when choosing insurance: the medical cost limit (at least USD 100,000 recommended), whether medical evacuation is covered, theft and loss compensation, the range of covered activities (diving, trekking, and so on), and whether pre-existing conditions are covered.

Health tip: On a long trip you will almost certainly experience "traveler's diarrhea." Always keep oral rehydration salts (ORS) on hand and stay thoroughly hydrated. Most cases clear up in two or three days, but if you have a fever of 38°C or higher or it lasts more than three days, be sure to visit a local hospital.

Money Management: Handling Cash Abroad

On a long trip, smart money management has a direct impact on the quality and length of your journey. You need to prepare in advance for a range of financial risks — losing a card, ATM fees, exchange-rate swings, and more.

Card Strategy: Spread Several Cards Around

Carry at least two or three cards for travel abroad and store them in separate places. Keep one in your wallet, one in your backpack, and one in the accommodation safe. That way, even if a card is lost or stolen, you can switch to another right away. Be sure to have at least one card with no foreign-transaction fees. Bringing one Visa and one Mastercard means you can cope even when one of them is not accepted somewhere.

Cutting ATM Fees

Withdrawing cash from an ATM abroad costs roughly USD 2.50–4 per transaction. To reduce this, it helps to withdraw a large amount at once — but carrying a lot of cash raises the risk of loss or theft. You have to find the right balance. Some bank cards waive foreign ATM fees up to a set number of times per month, so check before you leave. The Wise card is a popular choice among long-term travelers for its low foreign ATM fees and favorable exchange rates.

Using a Budget-Tracking App

If you do not track your spending on a long trip, it is easy to blow past your budget. Travel-specific budget apps such as TravelSpend and Trail Wallet let you manage spending in multiple currencies on a single screen. Get into the habit of logging your expenses every day and checking whether your average daily spending stays within the budget you planned. Communication: Staying in Touch With Family and Managing Time Zones

Keeping in touch with family and friends during a long trip is very important for your emotional stability. But with time differences and a busy schedule, it is easy to let contact slip.

Set up a regular contact schedule in advance. If you fix a specific time — for example, "a video call every Sunday at 8 p.m. home time" — both sides can keep that slot free. Creating a group chat on KakaoTalk or LINE to share a few photos and quick updates with family and close friends reduces the burden of contacting everyone individually.

For managing time zones, the time-difference feature in the myTravel app comes in handy. You can check the local time at your destination and your home time side by side, making it easy to work out a good moment to get in touch. On a long trip that moves between several countries, the time difference keeps changing, so a tool like this is especially valuable.

Do not forget to share your safety, either. If you set up Google Maps location sharing with your family, they can see your current location in real time, which eases their worry. Especially when traveling alone, checking in with your family once a day is not optional but essential.

Planning a Long-Term Route: A Look at Popular Itineraries

Planning the route for a long trip is the most enjoyable part of the process — and, at the same time, the part you agonize over most. Referring to proven, popular routes can be a big help in shaping your plan.

Southeast Asia Loop (1–3 months)

This is the route most recommended for newcomers to long-term travel. Prices are low, the infrastructure is well developed, and there is a lively traveler community. A classic route is Bangkok (Thailand) → Chiang Mai (Thailand) → Luang Prabang (Laos) → Hanoi (Vietnam) → Hoi An (Vietnam) → Ho Chi Minh City (Vietnam) → Phnom Penh (Cambodia) → Siem Reap (Cambodia) → Bangkok (Thailand) → Bali (Indonesia). Staying five to ten days in each city makes it roughly a two- to three-month route. Cheap flights within Southeast Asia are another plus, letting you adjust the route flexibly.

European Rail Route (1–2 months)

A European rail journey using a Eurail pass is the stuff of long-term travel dreams. Paris (France) → Brussels (Belgium) → Amsterdam (Netherlands) → Berlin (Germany) → Prague (Czechia) → Vienna (Austria) → Budapest (Hungary) → the Croatian coast → Italy (Venice → Florence → Rome) → Barcelona (Spain) → Lisbon (Portugal) is a typical route. Mixing Western and Eastern Europe helps balance your budget. What you save in Eastern Europe you can spend more freely in the West.

South America Route (2–4 months)

This is a route for the more adventurous traveler. Bogotá (Colombia) → Medellín (Colombia) → Quito (Ecuador) → the Galápagos (Ecuador) → Lima (Peru) → Cusco/Machu Picchu (Peru) → La Paz (Bolivia) → Uyuni (Bolivia) → Santiago (Chile) → Patagonia (Argentina/Chile) → Buenos Aires (Argentina). South America covers vast distances, so travel eats up a lot of time and you need a generous schedule. Taking overnight buses lets you save on both time and lodging at once.

Route tip: When planning a long-term route, a "relaxed schedule" is far better than a "packed" one. Build in the flexibility to stay longer if a city turns out better than expected. We recommend generating a rough route with myTravel and then adjusting it freely as you travel.

Coming Home After a Long Trip: Returning to Everyday Life

The process of readjusting after a long trip is something a surprising number of travelers find difficult. It is called "reverse culture shock," and it is a completely natural phenomenon.

Reverse culture shock is the psychological discomfort you feel when you return to a regimented daily routine after getting used to the freedom of life on the road. You may find yourself thinking, "Why do I have to go to work at the same time every day?" or "Why do I have to live this busily?" This is a normal reaction, and most people adjust naturally within two to four weeks.

As for tips for returning, do not go straight home on the last day of your trip — give yourself a day or two of breathing room. Going straight back to work after landing makes the abrupt switch far more stressful. Taking time to wrap up the trip by organizing the photos and notes you gathered along the way makes the psychological transition smoother. Carrying an interest you newly discovered on the road (cooking, languages, photography, and so on) into your everyday life helps you keep the trip's positive energy alive.

A financial review is also something you must do after returning. Tally up all your spending from the trip, check your card statements, and see whether there is anything you need to claim a refund or insurance for. This data becomes a valuable reference for budgeting your next long trip.

In Closing: For Long-Term Travel, Now Is the Best Time

You do not set off on a long trip only after you are perfectly prepared. The moment you are 100% ready never comes. If you are about 70–80% prepared, you can fill in the rest as you travel. The very process of solving unexpected situations is part of the journey — and an experience that helps you grow.

Use the basics covered in this guide — budgeting, minimalist packing, accommodation strategy, health care, money management, and route planning — as a reference, but adjust them to fit your own style. The biggest lesson of long-term travel is that "it's okay when things don't go according to plan." Design your basic route and itinerary with myTravel and revise it freely as you go. A month from now, you will surely be a different person from the one who set out.
