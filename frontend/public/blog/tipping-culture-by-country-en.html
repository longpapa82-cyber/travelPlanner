A Complete Guide to Tipping Culture by Country: How Much, When, and How

2026-08-12 | Hoonjae Park · myTravel Travel Content Team

Have you ever received the bill at a destination and suddenly felt your mind spin? "Am I supposed to tip here? And if so, how much?" Tipping culture varies completely from country to country. In some places, failing to tip makes you seem rude; in others, tipping is actually taken as a slight to the person receiving it.

This article outlines the broad patterns of tipping culture by country, focusing on the US, Europe, and Asia, along with situational guidelines for restaurants, hotels, taxis, and tours. First, though, please keep in mind that tipping is mostly a custom rather than a legal obligation, and that even within the same country, practices vary widely by city, industry, and situation. The amounts here are only general reference points.

1. What Is Tipping Culture, and Why Does It Exist?

A tip is a small token of appreciation handed to someone who has provided a service, on top of the actual charge. There are various theories about the origin of the word, but at its heart it is "an expression of gratitude for good service." The catch is that this simple concept has taken root in entirely different ways from country to country.

The biggest factor dividing countries with strong tipping cultures from those with weak ones is the wage structure of service workers. In countries like the US, where a server's base pay is low and tips make up a large share of their real income, tips effectively function as part of their salary. On the other hand, in countries where wages are fully built into the prices, or where a service charge is added to the bill automatically, tips carry much less weight.

Three key concepts

  • Service Charge: An amount added to the bill automatically. If it's already included, a separate tip is usually unnecessary.
  • Tip / Gratuity: A voluntary token of appreciation given by the customer.
  • Rounding: A common practice in Europe of leaving small change or rounding the amount up when paying.

So when you're preparing for a trip, the most important thing is to first figure out whether your destination is a "culture that expects tips," a "culture where a service charge is included," or a "culture with no tipping at all." Just grasping these three axes will let you handle most situations.

Another thing worth knowing is that a tip can also function as "an assessment of the quality of service." In countries with strong tipping cultures, the size of the tip a customer leaves acts as unspoken feedback on the service. If you were very satisfied, you leave a generous amount; if you were disappointed, you leave less. That said, leaving no tip at all because the service fell short can be taken as a fairly strong signal of displeasure, so unless something was truly wrong, it's safest to at least meet the baseline level.

In short, tipping isn't simply a matter of money but a cultural agreement about how a society treats service labor and how it expresses gratitude. Even if a custom is unfamiliar, understanding the reasoning behind it lets you respond naturally to the situation instead of freezing up in front of the bill.

2. The US: The Country with the World's Strongest Tipping Culture

From a traveler's perspective, the country to pay the most attention to is unquestionably the US. Because tips go beyond a simple thank-you and form the core of service workers' income, skipping a tip without good reason can leave the impression that you're being rude.

When you're seated and served at a sit-down restaurant, the common practice is 15–20% of the pre-tax amount. If the service was average, 15%; if you were satisfied, 18–20%; if it was truly excellent, sometimes even more. When paying by card, the receipt often has a separate line for entering the tip.

A worked example: an $80 meal at a New York restaurant

Let's say the pre-tax food cost is $80. Since it's customary to calculate the tip based on the pre-tax amount, you can leave out the roughly $7 that comes from New York State sales tax (about 8.875%) when figuring the tip.

  • If the service was average, 15%: $80 × 0.15 = about $12
  • If you were satisfied, 18%: $80 × 0.18 = about $14–15
  • If it was truly excellent, 20%: $80 × 0.20 = $16

If mental math is hard, there's a simple trick. Shifting the decimal point of the pre-tax amount by one place (here, $8) is roughly 10%, and double that ($16) is 20%. Pick something between the two to match the service. In fact, in some areas like New York, "double the sales tax" ends up close to a 20% tip, so some people use the tax amount as a rough guide.

Caution: when it's already on the bill

For parties of six or more, or at restaurants in tourist areas, a "gratuity" or "service charge" (usually around 18%) may already be included in the bill. In that case, adding another tip means paying twice. Be sure to check the bottom of the bill for phrases like "gratuity included" or "18% service charge added for parties of 6 or more." If it's already there, just leave the tip line blank or write in 0.

There are plenty of situations beyond restaurants where you tip. Broken down further, here are the commonly accepted standards by situation. None of these are absolute rules — they're just widely used customs.

SituationCommon practice (US)
Sit-down restaurant server15–20% pre-tax
Bartender (per drink)$1–2 per drink, or 15–20% on the tab
Taxi driver10–15% of the fare (including rounding up the change)
Ride-hailing (Uber/Lyft)10–20% in-app; $1–2 for short trips
Hotel bellhop (carrying bags)$1–2 per bag
Housekeeping (room cleaning)$2–5 per day
Valet parking$2–5 when your car is brought back
Hair salon / nails / massage15–20% of the service cost
Food delivery10–15% or at least $3–5

With ride-hailing apps like Uber or Lyft, you can add a tip on the app screen after getting out. On the rating/tip screen that pops up right after the ride, you can pick a preset amount (e.g., 10%, 15%, 20%) or enter your own. Since it's handled inside the app, you don't need cash, and you can also add it later.

However, at cafés or fast-food places where you order and pick up at the counter yourself, a tip is not obligatory. Lately more tablet payment screens prompt you to tip, but at these self-service establishments, not tipping is not rude. Feel free to select "No tip" without hesitation.

To avoid getting stressed about tipping on a US trip, it helps to get familiar with the procedure for adding a tip when paying by card. The common sequence when paying by card at a sit-down restaurant goes like this.

  • The server first brings a receipt (check) listing only the food cost.
  • When you hand over your card, the server charges the food cost first, then brings back a receipt with a blank tip line (Tip / Gratuity) and a blank Total line.
  • Here you write in the tip amount you want by hand, write the sum of the food cost plus tip in the Total box, and sign.
  • There are usually two copies of the receipt (one for the restaurant, one for you). Fill in and sign the restaurant's copy, leave it on the table, and you're done.

Meanwhile, at cafés, fast-food places, or spots where you pay on the spot with a tablet terminal, buttons like 15%, 18%, or 20% appear on the screen and you press one, or choose "No tip / Custom." In this case the tip is finalized the moment you pay, so it's hard to change afterward. By contrast, the handwritten method at a sit-down restaurant lets you change the amount until you sign. If this process is unfamiliar, it can be flustering at first, so it helps to learn the rough calculation tricks introduced above in advance.

It's also common to tip at hair salons, nail shops, for massages, valet parking, delivery, and more. As a rule of thumb, the more personal the service, the higher the tip expectation. Conversely, you don't tip public services staffed by people on a fixed salary, such as airline employees or government offices. In summary, in the US it's safest to keep tips in mind whenever it's "a situation where a person is directly serving you."

So what happens if you don't tip? Since tipping isn't a legal obligation, you won't be punished or stopped for not doing it. That said, at places like sit-down restaurants where tips make up a large share of income, skipping the tip without good reason is easily read by the server as rude or as a sign of serious dissatisfaction with the service. At a place you frequent, it could even affect the atmosphere of service on your next visit. If the service really was bad, you can express your view by reducing the tip, but even then, letting the manager know about the problem is a more constructive response.

Recently, there's been a rise in tablet screens prompting tips even at self-service establishments and takeout counters — the so-called "tip-flation" phenomenon. Even when picking up a single cup of coffee, 18% and 20% buttons appear first. If you've decided not to tip in these situations, just press the "No tip" or "Custom → 0" button on the screen. Even if the cashier is watching, tipping isn't obligatory at self-service establishments, so there's no need to feel pressured. In other words, the key is to distinguish between a case where a person continuously waits on you, like table service, and a case where you pick things up yourself.

3. Europe: Service Charges Are Often Included, and Tips Are a Light Gesture

Europe has quite a different feel from the US. In many countries, labor costs are built into the prices, or a service charge is added to the bill automatically, so a tip is less an "obligation" and more "a light gesture offered when things were good." Overall, the common practice is to round up the amount or leave around 5–10%.

That said, there are differences in warmth even within Europe. The table below shows only general tendencies, and things can vary by region, restaurant tier, and situation.

CountryCommon restaurant practice
FranceThe service charge is often included in the price ("service compris"). If satisfied, round up the change or leave a small amount
ItalyA "coperto" (cover charge) is sometimes added. Tips aren't required — rounding up the change is enough
GermanyIt's common to round up the total and state an amount about 5–10% higher when paying
UKA service charge (usually 12.5%) may be added. If it isn't, around 10% is customary
SpainTipping culture is on the weaker side. Leaving the change is enough

How to tip the German way

In Germany, Austria, and similar places, when paying by card it's common to tell the staff the "total including the tip" out loud. For example, if the bill is 18 euros, you can say "20 euros, please (Zwanzig, bitte)." It's a culture of handling it at the moment of payment rather than leaving change on the table.

A real example: rounding up the change at a Paris café

Say you have coffee and a croissant at a Paris café and the bill comes to 8.60 euros. France is legally structured so that the service charge is included in the price ("service compris"), so a separate tip isn't obligatory. Still, if the service was good, people commonly do this.

  • Pay 10 euros in coins and leave the 1.40 euros in change behind, or
  • Round up lightly to about 9 euros, leaving 40 centimes.

If you had a more formal meal at a restaurant, people sometimes leave around 5% of the total. Either way, remember it's at the level of "leaving your change with a good feeling," clearly different from the American 15–20%.

As a common rule, in Europe you should first check whether a service charge is added to the bill. If you see wording like "service charge," "servizio," or "service compris," an additional tip is optional. Without feeling pressured, leaving a small amount as a gesture of thanks when the service was good is plenty.

There's one common misunderstanding when tipping in Europe: expecting a line on the receipt to write in the tip amount, American-style. European card terminals often have no tip entry field. In that case, it's natural to tell the staff the total out loud, as in the German method above, or to leave the exact amount in cash on the table. That's why, for European trips, it's handy to keep some coins and small bills on hand for giving tips.

Looking more closely at regional differences: Northern Europe (Sweden, Norway, Finland, Denmark, etc.) generally has a weak tipping culture, since labor costs are amply reflected in prices, so just rounding up the change is enough. In the UK, a service charge (usually around 12.5%) is often added automatically at restaurants, in which case an additional tip isn't needed. However, when you order and receive drinks directly at the counter in a pub, not tipping is actually the natural thing. In Italy, the first step is to check whether the bill includes a "coperto" (a per-person cover charge) or "servizio"; if it does, a separate tip is optional.

Conversely, at some tourist-area restaurants in southern Europe with lots of visitors, staff may quietly expect a tip, or present foreigners with a bill that already has a service charge added, so check the bottom carefully. One mistake European travelers often make is bringing their American 20% habit along unchanged. In most parts of Europe, 20% is an excessive amount, so the recipient may actually feel awkward or try to return the change. In the end, in Europe you won't go far wrong if you just remember the principle that "a tip is only a token of thanks, not an obligation," along with the sense of "round up the change or around 5–10%."

4. Asia: Countries with No Tipping and Countries That Expect It

Asia swings between extremes when it comes to tipping culture. It broadly splits into cultures with no tipping (where it's actually impolite) and cultures that expect tips, centered on the tourism industry.

Japan and Korea: No Tipping Culture

Japan and Korea are prime examples of countries with no tipping culture. Good service is regarded as something already included in the price and simply expected, and handing over a tip may leave the person flustered or even taken as impolite. In Japan, if you leave money on the table after paying, staff will often treat it as "something you left behind" and chase after you to return it. The common way of paying at restaurants (paying at a register near the entrance while holding the bill, rather than at the table) doesn't even create a place to leave a tip.

Korea, too, has virtually no custom of exchanging tips anywhere — restaurants, taxis, or hotels. In fact, if you try to leave your change as a tip in a taxi, the driver will often insist on giving you exact change. Of course, there are exceptions. When you receive attentive service at a high-end Japanese ryokan, there's an old custom of handing an envelope of "kokorozuke" to the room attendant (nakai-san) on arrival. But this belongs to a special realm of formality, and for ordinary travel, treating "no tipping" as the default is safe by any measure.

Don't tip in Japan or Korea

A tip is unnecessary anywhere — restaurants, taxis, or hotels. If you want to express gratitude, it's far more natural to convey it with polite words and manner rather than money. There are a few exceptional situations, such as high-end ryokan, but for ordinary travel you can keep "no tipping" as your default.

Southeast Asia: Small Tips Centered on Tourist Spots

Southeast Asia — Thailand, Vietnam, Indonesia, Cambodia, and so on — varies by country and situation. Tipping culture traditionally wasn't strong, but at hotels, spas, and tours in areas with many tourists, small tips have naturally taken hold. Here are some common situations as examples.

  • Spa / massage: After a 1–2 hour session, if you were satisfied, handing the therapist a single small-denomination local bill.
  • Hotel bag carrying / room cleaning: About one small bill. The bigger the resort, the more accustomed staff are to this custom.
  • Tours / drivers: In some areas, after a full day of guiding, it's natural to split a small amount between the guide and the driver.

By contrast, at local eateries, street stalls, convenience stores, and on public transport that locals mainly use, a tip is generally unnecessary. In other words, in Southeast Asia you keep small tips only for "services within the tourism infrastructure where a person waits on you directly," and you don't need to worry about them for everyday spending. That said, at high-end restaurants in tourist-heavy places like Bangkok or Bali, a service charge (usually around 10%) and tax may be added to the bill, so check the bottom.

The Middle East: Tips ("Baksheesh") Are Generally Expected

In parts of the Middle East and North Africa, such as Egypt and Jordan, a tipping custom called "baksheesh" is widespread. It's expected not only from hotels, restaurants, and guides, but also for minor help like assisting with a photo or giving directions. Travelers unfamiliar with this culture may find it a bit burdensome, but locally it's an established custom, a kind of mode of interaction. Keeping a comfortable supply of small notes and change lets you respond flexibly to each situation, and it's perfectly fine to politely decline excessive demands for help you didn't want. Knowing in advance that you may be asked for small amounts in unexpected places — restroom use, guides at tourist sites, and so on — will keep you from being caught off guard.

Greater China and Beyond

Mainland China, Hong Kong, and Taiwan — the Greater China region — also traditionally don't have a strong tipping culture. That said, a service charge may be added to the bill at places like high-end restaurants in Hong Kong, and small tips are sometimes accepted for services aimed at tourists. Since the finer customs differ by country, it's good to lightly check the recent situation in your destination before visiting.

In short, in Asia the key is to confirm in advance whether "a tip is expected in this country and this situation." Applying a one-size-fits-all approach can backfire. In particular, remember that in countries like Japan and Korea where not tipping is the polite thing, habitually handing over a tip can put the other person in an awkward spot — contrary to the goodwill you meant to show.

5. A Situational Tipping Guide: Restaurants, Hotels, Taxis, and Tours

Once you've grasped the broad patterns by country, it's time to learn the situational standards. The table below is based on general practice in countries with a tipping culture (especially the US); Europe is lower than this, and in Japan and Korea most of it doesn't apply.

SituationUS (strong tipping)Europe (generally)Japan / Korea
Sit-down restaurant15–20% pre-taxIncluded or 5–10%Not needed
Café / fast food (self-service)Optional (not needed)Round up the changeNot needed
Hotel bellhop$1–2 per bag1–2 eurosNot needed
Housekeeping$2–5 per dayOptional (small amount)Not needed
Taxi10–15% of the fareRound up the changeNot needed
Tour guide (half day / full day)$5–10+ per personSmall amount to 10%Not needed

For restaurants, as explained earlier, first check whether the bill includes a service charge. At hotels, the bellhop who carries your bags directly and the housekeeping staff who tidy your room are the ones you tip, and you can also hand a small amount to the front desk or concierge if they gave you special help.

How to decide on a tour guide tip

  • On a full-day tour with separate guide and driver, in some areas it's customary to split a small amount between the two.
  • On a group tour, participants sometimes each chip in a little at the end and hand it over together.
  • A free walking tour, despite the name, is often run on tips. If you were satisfied, showing your appreciation is the courteous thing to do.

Taxis vary widely by country. In the US, 10–15% of the fare is common; in Europe it's at the level of rounding up the change; and in Japan and Korea it's not needed. App-based ride-hailing services sometimes have a feature to add a tip within the app, so check the payment screen.

One thing to keep in mind when reading the table is that these figures are reference customs, not absolute rules. Even within the US, the atmosphere differs between large cities and small towns, and between upscale and casual restaurants. In Europe, too, the amount a customer adds actually changes depending on whether a service charge is included. So rather than memorizing numbers, it's more practical to first get a sense of the big-picture direction — "does this country tend to expect tips or not" — and adjust the specific amounts flexibly to fit the situation.

To add one more practical tip: if you plan to tip in various situations, secure plenty of small bills and coins early in your trip. This reduces situations where you can't tip because you only have large bills, or where it becomes awkward to get change back. For cases like hotel housekeeping where you leave a small amount daily, it's convenient to portion out a day's worth in advance.

6. A Tipping Mistake Checklist and Closing Thoughts

Finally, here's a summary of the tipping mistakes travelers often run into and how to avoid them. Just following the big principles will prevent most awkward situations.

  • Check for double payment: Be sure to check the bottom of the bill for whether a service charge is already included.
  • Prepare small cash: For bellhops, housekeeping, taxis, and the like, it's handy to have small bills. There are many situations where cards can't be used.
  • Pre-tax basis: US restaurant tip percentages are customarily calculated on the pre-tax amount.
  • Respect no-tip countries: In places like Japan and Korea, not tipping is actually the polite thing.
  • Don't feel pressured: Tablet tip prompts at self-service establishments are optional. Don't feel obliged.
  • Check local customs in advance: For places with region-specific customs like baksheesh in the Middle East, learn about them beforehand.

Among the items on the checklist above, the point travelers especially often stumble on is "applying one country's habits directly to another country." Bringing the American 20% habit unchanged to Europe or Asia results in overspending, or in a no-tip country, it's actually impolite. Conversely, habitually skipping the tip in a country with a strong tipping culture can leave a rude impression on service workers. Getting into the habit of briefly reviewing "which type is this place?" each time your destination changes is a big help.

A few common misconceptions are also worth pointing out. First, the misconception that "a tip should be calculated on the tax-included amount." At US restaurants, it's customary to calculate on the pre-tax amount, so multiplying the percentage by the total including sales tax means you pay a bit more. Second, the misconception that "the more you tip, the more polite it is." Handing over an excessive amount in a country where tipping is weak or nonexistent can actually put the other person in an awkward spot. Third, the misconception that "if you only pay by card, you can't tip." In most of the US and Europe you can handle tips via a card terminal, receipt, or app, but there are also situations where cash is far more natural, such as with bellhops, housekeeping, or restroom attendants, so it's safest to always keep a few small notes on hand.

Finally, the automatic gratuity added to the bill is another frequently confusing part. In the US, reservations for parties of six or more and some restaurants in tourist areas charge a service fee automatically; in this case a tip is already included, so adding another tip means paying twice. Conversely, if you want to voluntarily add more for satisfying service, you can add a small amount on top, but it isn't required. The point is to make it a habit to give the bill a quick once-over every time you pay. This one short check prevents most unnecessary spending and awkward misunderstandings.

The heart of tipping culture is, in the end, "consideration and gratitude toward others." Rather than feeling burdened to perfectly follow a set right answer, an attitude of respecting the customs of that country matters more. In countries with strong tipping, tip with an understanding of service workers' livelihoods; in countries with no tipping, express your gratitude with polite words and manner.

If it's a hassle to look up the different tipping standards for each destination every time, organizing destination-specific information with an app like myTravel, which lets you manage your itinerary and budget together, can help when you're planning. For a well-prepared traveler, both the hesitation in front of the bill and the awkward moments will be far fewer. Have a wonderful trip!
