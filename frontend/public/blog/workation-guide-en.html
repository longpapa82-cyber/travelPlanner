The Complete Workation Guide: How to Work and Travel at the Same Time

2026-07-10 | myTravel Team

What a Workation Actually Is (and Isn't)

A workation is a working vacation: you keep your regular job and hours, but you do them from somewhere you'd rather be. It's not a two-week beach holiday, and it's not a full digital-nomad lifestyle where your entire life is packed into a backpack. It sits in the middle — you're accountable to your team from 9 to 5, and the mountains, the ocean, or a new city start after you close your laptop.

The mistake most first-timers make is treating a workation like a vacation with a laptop attached. That leads to blown deadlines, resentful teammates, and a trip you never actually enjoyed because you were always half-working. The better mental model is: your workday moves, your work standards don't. If you deliver the same output you would from your desk at home, everything else is upside.

Before you book anything, be honest about two things. First, how much focused work do you genuinely need to do each day — four hours or eight? Second, are your working hours flexible, or are you locked to meetings in your home time zone? Those two answers shape every other decision below, from the destination to the neighborhood you stay in.

Choosing the Right Destination

The best workation destination isn't the most exotic one — it's the one that removes friction from your workday. A stunning island with unreliable power and a five-hour time gap will sabotage you faster than a modest mid-sized city with fiber internet.

Weigh these factors, roughly in this order of importance for most remote workers:

  • Time zone overlap. If your team is in New York and you're in Bali, your afternoon is their pre-dawn. Aim for at least 3-4 hours of overlap with the people you sync with most. For US-based workers, Mexico, Costa Rica, and Colombia are gold because they share or nearly share your working hours.
  • Internet reliability. Not just "is there Wi-Fi" — is there fast, stable Wi-Fi with a backup option. More on this below.
  • Cost of living. A workation can actually be cheaper than staying home if you pick a lower-cost region and your income stays the same.
  • Safety and healthcare. You're staying weeks, not days. Check the neighborhood, not just the country.
  • The "after work" payoff. The whole point is what happens after you log off. Is there something you'll actually want to walk out the door for?

Internet and Your Workspace Setup

Your internet connection is the single point of failure that can turn a workation into a disaster. Treat redundancy as non-negotiable.

Build a two-layer connection plan

  • Primary: the accommodation's Wi-Fi. Before booking, message the host and ask for an actual speed-test screenshot, not just a promise. You want at least 20-30 Mbps down and 10 Mbps up for smooth video calls.
  • Backup: a local SIM or eSIM with a generous data plan, so you can hotspot from your phone the moment the main connection drops mid-meeting. An eSIM you activate before you land saves you a stressful first day.

The physical workspace

A bed is not a desk. Two days of hunching over a laptop on a mattress and your neck will file a complaint. Prioritize accommodations with a real table and chair, or scout co-working spaces nearby. Co-working spaces are worth the daily fee for three reasons: reliable internet, ergonomic seating, and a psychological line between "work mode" and "travel mode."

Pack the small things that punch above their weight: a lightweight laptop stand, a compact external keyboard and mouse, noise-cancelling earbuds for calls in noisy cafes, and a universal power adapter. These fit in a daypack and transform any table into a workable desk.

Managing Time Zones and Your Schedule

Time zones are where good intentions quietly fall apart. The trick is to design your schedule before you travel, not to improvise it once jet lag hits.

Map the overlap

Write down your core collaboration hours — the block when you truly need to be reachable — and translate them to local time at your destination. If your team's 10am-2pm sync window lands at 6pm-10pm where you're going, decide now whether you can live with evening meetings or whether that destination is off the table.

Communicate proactively

  • Tell your team your working hours in their time zone, clearly, before you leave. Ambiguity breeds frustration.
  • Set your calendar and chat status to reflect local hours so no one schedules a call at your midnight.
  • Over-communicate progress. When you're physically distant, visible output builds the trust that a shared office normally provides for free.

Beat the jet lag before it beats your Monday

Arrive a day or two before your first workday if you can. Trying to nail a big presentation on four hours of sleep and a wrecked circadian rhythm is a bad bet. Shift your sleep schedule by an hour or two in the days before departure, get sunlight on arrival, and hold off on heavy meals late at night.

Visas, Legal Rules, and the Boring-but-Critical Stuff

This is the least glamorous section and the one that can end your trip early. Working remotely from another country is a legal gray area in many places, and the rules genuinely matter.

Tourist visa vs. digital nomad visa

A standard tourist visa often technically does not permit "work," even remote work for a foreign employer. In practice, short stays for foreign-paid remote work are widely tolerated, but the risk is real for longer stays. That's why a growing list of countries now offer dedicated digital nomad visas — Portugal, Spain, Estonia, Croatia, Costa Rica, and dozens more — designed exactly for people doing your kind of trip. These typically require proof of remote income above a threshold and grant stays of six months to a couple of years.

Do this checklist before you commit

  • Confirm the maximum visa-free stay for your passport at the destination.
  • Check whether a digital nomad visa exists and whether you meet the income requirement.
  • Understand the tax implications — staying past a certain number of days (often 183) can trigger tax residency.
  • Clear it with your employer. Some companies restrict which countries you're allowed to work from for legal and security reasons.
  • Sort out travel and health insurance that covers you for the full stay.

Putting It All Together: A Sample First Workation

Here's how the pieces fit into a realistic four-week trip for someone on US Eastern time.

  1. Destination: Mexico City or Medellín — strong time zone overlap, reliable internet, rich food and culture for the after-work hours.
  2. Duration: Four weeks. Long enough to settle into a rhythm and offset the flight cost, short enough to avoid deep visa and tax complications.
  3. Week one: Arrive a weekend early. Get your local eSIM working, speed-test the apartment Wi-Fi, and scout one co-working space and two reliable cafes as backups.
  4. Working days: Same hours as home, treated with the same discipline. Explore in the evenings and go bigger on weekends.
  5. Weekends: This is your reward — a day trip, a hike, a neighborhood you've been curious about. Plan these ahead so you don't waste Saturday morning deciding.

Start smaller than you think. A well-run four-week workation close to your time zone teaches you far more than a chaotic three-month one on the other side of the planet. Nail the fundamentals — connection, schedule, legality — and the travel part takes care of itself.
