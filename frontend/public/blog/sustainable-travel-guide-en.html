Sustainable Travel Guide: How to See the World Without Costing the Earth

2026-07-10 | myTravel Team

What Sustainable Travel Actually Means

Sustainable travel isn't about giving up the trips you love or feeling guilty at every airport gate. It's about making a handful of informed choices that add up to a much lighter footprint on the places you visit. Tourism accounts for roughly 8% of global greenhouse gas emissions, and the vast majority of that comes from just two things: how we get there and how we get around once we arrive.

The good news is that the same decisions that lower your impact often make for richer, more memorable trips. Slower itineraries mean less time in transit and more time actually experiencing a place. Spending your money with local families instead of international chains puts you in restaurants and neighborhoods most tourists never see. Traveling in the shoulder season means fewer crowds and lower prices.

Think of sustainability as three overlapping goals: reducing your carbon footprint, supporting the local economy, and avoiding the strain of overtourism. You don't have to be perfect at all three on every trip. Picking even one or two things from each category below already puts you ahead of the average traveler.

Cutting Your Carbon Footprint on the Way There

Transportation is where the biggest wins live. A single long-haul flight can emit more CO2 per passenger than some people produce in a whole month of daily commuting. That doesn't mean you should never fly, but it does mean the way you fly matters.

Fly Smarter, Not Just Less

  • Choose direct routes. Takeoff and landing burn the most fuel, so a nonstop flight is almost always greener than one with a connection, even if the total distance is similar.
  • Fly economy. A business-class seat takes up roughly three times the space of an economy seat, and its carbon share is proportionally higher.
  • Pack lighter. Every kilogram on board costs fuel. Trimming 5 kg from your suitcase is a small but real contribution across a full flight.
  • Stay longer. One two-week trip is far lighter than two separate week-long trips to similar distances. Fewer flights, deeper experiences.

Consider the Ground Alternatives

For shorter distances, trains and buses can cut emissions by 80% or more compared with flying. A high-speed train between major cities is often faster once you factor in airport security and transfers, and you arrive in the city center instead of a distant airport. In many regions, an overnight train doubles as your accommodation for the night.

Getting Around Greener Once You Arrive

Local transport adds up quickly over a trip. The difference between a week of taxis and a week of walking, cycling, and public transit can be substantial, both for the planet and your wallet.

  • Walk the walkable parts. Many historic city centers are compact by design. Walking reveals side streets, cafés, and small shops you'd blow right past in a car.
  • Master the local transit pass. Metro, tram, and bus day-passes are usually a fraction of taxi costs and run on infrastructure that's already there. A single day-pass in most major cities costs less than one short cab ride.
  • Rent a bike or use bike-share. Cities from Amsterdam to Taipei have excellent bike networks, and a day rental often runs under the equivalent of a couple of coffees.
  • If you drive, share it. A rental car split among four people is dramatically more efficient per person than solo driving, and an electric or hybrid rental cuts that further.

Batch your sightseeing geographically. Instead of hitting a museum in the north in the morning and one in the south in the afternoon, cluster nearby stops together. You'll cover the same list with a fraction of the movement.

Spending in Ways That Actually Help Locals

Where your travel budget lands makes an enormous difference to the community hosting you. Studies of tourism spending have found that when travelers stay in international chains and eat at global franchises, a large share of the money leaves the local economy almost immediately. Spend it locally, and it circulates through families, small businesses, and neighborhoods.

Simple Swaps That Keep Money Local

  • Stay in locally owned guesthouses and small hotels instead of defaulting to international chains. You'll often get better recommendations and a more genuine sense of the place.
  • Eat where locals eat. Family-run restaurants, neighborhood markets, and street-food stalls put your money directly into the community and almost always taste better than tourist-strip menus.
  • Buy crafts from the makers. A handmade item bought directly from an artisan supports a real livelihood; the same-looking souvenir at the airport rarely does.
  • Book local guides. A resident-led walking tour spreads income to the community and gives you context you'd never get from a guidebook.

Avoiding Overtourism (and Enjoying Your Trip More)

Overtourism is what happens when too many visitors concentrate in the same few places at the same times. Historic centers get overwhelmed, housing costs rise for residents, and the very things that made a destination special start to erode. The frustrating part for travelers is that crowded, over-touristed experiences are usually the least enjoyable ones anyway.

Timing Is Everything

  • Travel in shoulder season. The weeks just before and after peak season often bring pleasant weather, thinner crowds, and noticeably lower prices on flights and lodging.
  • Visit popular sights early or late. The most famous landmarks are calm at opening time and again in the last hour before closing. Midday is when the tour buses arrive.
  • Explore beyond the highlight reel. For every world-famous city drowning in visitors, there's a smaller town nearby offering a similar experience with room to breathe.

Respect the Places You Visit

  • Follow local customs on dress and behavior, especially at religious and cultural sites.
  • Stick to marked trails in natural areas so you don't damage fragile ecosystems.
  • Take your waste with you and carry a reusable water bottle and shopping bag to cut down on single-use plastic.

Building a Realistic Low-Impact Trip

The biggest mistake well-meaning travelers make is trying to do everything perfectly and then giving up when it feels overwhelming. Sustainable travel is a direction, not a purity test. A trip that's 20% greener than your last one is a genuine win.

Here's a practical way to approach your next trip:

  1. Pick one big lever. Usually that's transportation. Choose a direct flight, add a train leg, or extend your stay so you're flying less often overall.
  2. Choose local for at least half your spending. Aim to eat, sleep, or shop locally more often than not. You don't have to be strict about every purchase.
  3. Build slack into your schedule. Over-packed itineraries force rushed transport and skipped experiences. Leaving gaps lets you walk more and linger longer.
  4. Plan geographically, not just chronologically. Group each day's stops by area to minimize backtracking.
