How to Find Travel Photo Spots That Actually Look Good

2026-07-10 | Hoonjae Park · myTravel Travel Content Team

Great Travel Photos Start Before You Pack

Most people plan a trip around where to eat and where to sleep, then treat photos as something that just happens along the way. Then they get home, scroll through 400 blurry, backlit, crowd-filled images, and keep maybe three. The photos that make you stop scrolling are almost never accidents. They are the result of two things: knowing exactly where to stand, and showing up at the right hour.

The good news is that both of those are plannable. You do not need a professional camera or years of practice. You need a short list of specific locations, a rough idea of when the light will be good, and a habit of arriving before everyone else. This guide walks through how to build that list and use it, from research all the way to framing the shot.

Think of it as a small layer on top of your normal itinerary. If you already map out your days in myTravel, you are most of the way there. The trick is treating a handful of photo spots as real stops with real times, not vague intentions.

Researching Spots Before You Go

The difference between a tourist photo and a memorable one is often just knowing the exact vantage point. That famous shot of a temple framed by a stone archway, or a skyline reflected in a specific canal, usually comes from one narrow spot that thousands of photos share. Your job before the trip is to collect those spots.

Where to actually look

  • Instagram location tags and hashtags. Search the destination name plus a landmark, then tap the location tag to see the exact geo-pinned spot. This tells you not just what a place looks like, but where photographers physically stood.
  • Google Maps photos. Open any landmark and scroll the user-submitted images. You will quickly see which angles repeat, and Maps gives you the precise coordinates to navigate back to.
  • Pinterest boards. Better for mood and composition ideas than exact locations, but useful for deciding what style of shot you are chasing.
  • YouTube walking tours. A 20-minute walkthrough of a neighborhood reveals corners and viewpoints that never make the highlight reels.

Aim for a realistic number. For a five-day trip, a shortlist of 8 to 12 spots is plenty. More than that and you spend the whole trip chasing photos instead of experiencing the place.

Save spots where your itinerary already lives. As you research, drop each photo location into the relevant day in myTravel as an activity, with a short note like "east corner of the bridge, sunrise." When the spots sit inside the same plan as your restaurants and museums, you naturally route through them instead of forgetting they existed.

Golden Hour Is Not a Suggestion

If you remember one thing from this guide, make it this: light matters more than location. The same street corner looks flat and harsh at noon and cinematic at 6 pm. Professional photographers obsess over timing because it is the single biggest lever they have.

The windows worth planning around

  • Golden hour is roughly the first hour after sunrise and the last hour before sunset. The light is warm, soft, and low, which flatters faces, buildings, and landscapes alike. This is your prime shooting window.
  • Blue hour is the 20 to 30 minutes just after sunset (or before sunrise), when the sky turns deep blue and city lights start to glow. It is ideal for skylines and bridges, where you get both ambient sky color and lit-up buildings.
  • Midday is the hardest light: harsh shadows, squinting subjects, blown-out skies. If you must shoot then, look for open shade or head indoors to markets, cafes, and covered arcades.

Golden and blue hour shift with the season and your latitude. In summer at high latitudes, sunset can land near 9 pm; in winter it might be 4:30 pm. Check the actual sunrise and sunset times for your dates and pin them, because a 30-minute miss can mean the difference between magic and flat gray.

Build your day around the light, not the other way around. When you lay out activities by time in myTravel, schedule your top viewpoint for the golden-hour slot and fill the harsh midday hours with indoor stops like museums or lunch. A little sequencing turns "we happened to be there" into "we planned to be there when it looked its best."

Beating the Crowds

Even a perfectly lit, perfectly framed spot is ruined by a wall of other tourists. The fix is almost always about timing, and it usually means being uncomfortably early.

Practical crowd-avoidance tactics

  1. Arrive at opening, or before. For major landmarks, the first 30 to 60 minutes after opening are dramatically emptier than the rest of the day. A sunrise shot at a popular bridge or square can look like you had the whole place to yourself.
  2. Go on weekdays. Saturdays and Sundays roughly double foot traffic at big attractions. If your schedule is flexible, save the icons for a Tuesday or Wednesday.
  3. Use the "popular times" graph. Google Maps shows an hour-by-hour busyness estimate for most landmarks. Aim for the visible dips.
  4. Shoot the shoulder seasons. Late spring and early autumn give you decent weather with a fraction of the peak-summer crowds.

There is a real trade-off here: sunrise shoots mean early alarms and rearranged mornings. But an empty frame at dawn is worth far more than a packed one at noon, and the light is better anyway. The two goals reinforce each other.

Framing and Composition Basics

You have the spot and the light. Now the actual shot. You do not need to memorize photography theory, but a few simple habits will lift your photos immediately.

Five rules that do most of the work

  • Rule of thirds. Turn on your phone's grid (in the camera settings) and place your subject or the horizon along the lines, not dead center. Instantly more balanced.
  • Leading lines. Use roads, railings, rivers, or rows of trees to draw the eye toward your subject. A path curving into the frame gives depth.
  • Foreground layers. Include something close, like a flower, a doorway, or a railing, to add a sense of depth instead of a flat postcard.
  • Frame within a frame. Shoot through an archway, a window, or overhanging branches to focus attention and add context.
  • Get low or get high. Crouching or finding a slight elevation change removes distractions and makes ordinary scenes feel intentional.

Quick phone-camera settings

Tap to set focus and exposure on your subject. Drag the exposure slider down slightly when shooting bright skies so they do not blow out. Avoid digital zoom, which just crops and degrades your image, and step closer with your feet instead. Clean your lens with your shirt before every serious shot, because a smudged lens is the most common reason travel photos look hazy.

Turning a Shot List Into a Real Plan

All of this only pays off if it lives inside your actual itinerary instead of a scattered notes app. The workflow that consistently works looks like this:

  1. Research your 8 to 12 spots and note the exact vantage point for each.
  2. Check sunrise and sunset times for your travel dates.
  3. Assign each spot to a day and a specific time window, weighting your favorites toward golden or blue hour.
  4. Slot indoor and low-stakes activities into the harsh midday hours.
  5. Leave buffer time, because the best photos often come from lingering an extra 15 minutes for the light to shift.

Photography rewards preparation more than gear. A specific spot, the right hour, an early alarm, and a couple of composition habits will do more for your travel photos than any lens upgrade. Do the light-and-location homework once, fold it into your plan, and you come home with images worth keeping.
