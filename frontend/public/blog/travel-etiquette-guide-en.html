A Travel Etiquette Guide: Cultural Manners That Vary by Country

2026-07-25 | myTravel Team

An 'I Didn't Know' Mistake Is Still a Mistake

Behavior that feels natural to us can be read as rude locally. Even with no bad intent, not knowing a culture's manners can make someone uncomfortable without meaning to. Conversely, knowing and keeping one small courtesy can visibly change how locals treat you.

Travel etiquette isn't memorizing rules — it's an attitude of respect toward the people of a country. This guide lays out the broad patterns of how manners differ across six areas: greetings, dining, dress, tipping, religious sites, and photography.

Greetings and Gestures: Same Action, Different Meaning

Greetings vary widely by culture. Handshakes, light hugs, cheek kisses, pressed palms, a slight bow — the default differs by region. When meeting someone for the first time, it's safest to follow whatever they do first.

Watch your hand gestures. A gesture that's nothing to us can be an insult somewhere. Notably, pointing at people with a finger, handing things with the left hand, patting someone's head, or pointing at objects with your foot are considered rude in certain cultures. Searching "(country) cultural manners for travelers" before you go helps you avoid big blunders.

Dining and Dress: Follow the Country's Rules

Dining etiquette differs by country. Eating with sound is polite in some places and rude in others, and utensil use, toasting customs, and paying culture all vary. In a local restaurant, observe what those around you do and follow suit — that's generally safe.

Dress matters especially at religious sites. Conservative cultures and places of worship often require covering the shoulders and knees. Packing a thin scarf or a long garment is useful when you suddenly need to enter a temple or church. Even in a beach city with relaxed dress, it's better to be modest downtown or at religious sites.

Tipping: Completely Different by Country

Tipping is what travelers find most confusing — because it's completely different by country.

In some countries tipping is effectively expected: not giving a set percentage (say 15–20%) at restaurants, taxis, and hotels reads as rude. In others there's no tipping culture at all, and a tip may make people awkward or lead them to refuse it. Elsewhere a service charge is already built into the bill. It's worth learning the tipping norms of the country you're visiting, and keeping small cash handy so you're not caught out when a tip is expected.

Religious Sites and Photography

Temples, churches, and mosques are sacred spaces before they're attractions. Follow the rules: places where you remove your shoes, areas you can't enter, and spots where you must be quiet. Signs at the entrance or the behavior of locals will guide you.

Photography needs manners too. Ask permission before photographing people — especially children, religious rituals, and vendors. Always obey no-photography signs and military sites; in some countries, taking photos can be a legal issue. For capturing beautiful shots, see the travel photo spots guide.

Wrap-Up: Respect Is the Best Etiquette

You don't need to memorize every rule of travel etiquette. The core is one thing: first ask "what's the right way to do this here?", and when unsure, observe locals and follow. Knowing just these six — greetings, dining, dress, tipping, religious sites, photography — avoids most mistakes.
