Tokyo in 4 Days: A First-Timer's 3-Night Itinerary

2026-07-10 | myTravel Team

Before You Go: The Groundwork That Saves Hours

Tokyo rewards a little prep. It is a sprawling city where two neighborhoods that look close on a map can be a 40-minute train ride apart, so the biggest first-timer mistake is zigzagging across the metro all day. This itinerary groups each day around one or two adjacent hubs so you spend your energy walking and eating, not transferring lines.

Two things to sort out before you land:

  • Suica or PASMO IC card: Load one via Apple Wallet or Google Wallet before your trip, or grab a physical card at the airport. You tap in and out of every train, bus, and most convenience stores. Budget around 3,000-5,000 yen of top-up per travel day.
  • Pocket Wi-Fi or an eSIM: An eSIM for a 4-day trip runs roughly 2,000-4,000 yen and saves you hunting for free Wi-Fi. You will lean on maps and translation constantly.

Day 1: Asakusa, the Old Tokyo Warm-Up

Land, drop your bags, and start in Asakusa. Jet lag is real, so day one is deliberately gentle and walkable.

Morning to midday

  • Senso-ji Temple: Tokyo's oldest temple. Enter through the giant red Kaminarimon lantern gate and walk Nakamise-dori, the market street leading to the main hall. Go before 10am if you can; by noon it is shoulder to shoulder.
  • Street snacks: Grab a freshly grilled ningyo-yaki (small filled cakes), a menchi-katsu, or a melon pan along Nakamise. Expect 200-500 yen per item.

Afternoon

  • Walk 15 minutes to the Sumida River and cross toward Tokyo Skytree. The observation deck is optional (about 2,100 yen), but the Solamachi mall at its base is a great, low-effort place to eat and rest.
  • If you have energy, wander back through Kappabashi "Kitchen Town," where restaurant-supply shops sell Japanese knives and the plastic food models you see in restaurant windows.

Evening

Stay local for dinner. Asakusa is full of unpretentious izakaya and tempura counters. Turn in early, because tomorrow is a full day.

Day 2: Shibuya and Harajuku, the Youth-Culture Core

Today is the Tokyo you have seen in movies. Two adjacent neighborhoods, one JR Yamanote stop apart, so you can do the whole day mostly on foot.

Morning: Harajuku

  • Meiji Jingu: A serene forest shrine right next to the Harajuku chaos. The contrast is the point. Free to enter; allow an hour.
  • Takeshita-dori: The pedestrian street for crepes, rainbow cotton candy, and teen fashion. Loud and fun for 30 minutes.
  • Omotesando: Walk south into the tree-lined "Champs-Elysees of Tokyo" for architecture and cafes if you want calmer surroundings.

Afternoon and evening: Shibuya

  • Shibuya Crossing: The famous scramble. Watch it from the second-floor Starbucks or the free viewpoint at Shibuya Sky's building.
  • Shibuya Sky: Book the sunset slot ahead (about 2,500 yen). The open-air rooftop deck is one of the best skyline views in the city.
  • Dinner: Shibuya's back streets are packed with ramen, yakiniku, and standing bars. Ichiran-style tonkotsu ramen runs about 1,000-1,300 yen.

Day 3: Shinjuku, Gardens by Day, Neon by Night

Shinjuku is Tokyo at full volume. Its station is the busiest in the world, so give yourself buffer time to navigate it.

Morning

  • Shinjuku Gyoen: A large, calm garden (about 500 yen entry). A perfect slow start with Japanese, English, and French landscaped sections.
  • Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building: Free observation decks on the 45th floor. On a clear day you can spot Mount Fuji to the west.

Afternoon

  • Explore the department stores and electronics floors around the east exit, or take a short hop to Ikebukuro or Nakano Broadway if you are into anime and collectibles.

Evening

  • Omoide Yokocho: A cluster of tiny post-war alley eateries serving yakitori and beer. Smoky, cramped, and full of character.
  • Golden Gai: Six alleys of miniature bars, each seating a handful of people. Note that some have a small cover charge; look for English-friendly signs.
  • Kabukicho: The neon entertainment district, worth a walk-through even if you just want the lights.

Day 4: A Flexible Finale Near Tokyo Station

Your last day depends on flight timing, so keep it modular and close to your onward transport.

If you have a full morning

  • Tsukiji Outer Market: The inner fish market moved to Toyosu, but the outer market still buzzes with fresh sushi, tamagoyaki skewers, and knife shops. Go hungry.
  • Ginza: A short walk away for flagship stores and the quieter side streets. On weekends the main avenue turns pedestrian-only in the afternoon.
  • Imperial Palace East Gardens: Free, green, and a calm bookend to a busy trip.

Getting to the airport

  • Narita: The Narita Express from Tokyo Station takes about 60 minutes. Leave at least 3.5 hours before departure.
  • Haneda: Much closer, roughly 30-45 minutes by train from central Tokyo. A 3-hour buffer is usually plenty.

Budget Guide: What 4 Days Actually Costs

Tokyo can be as cheap or as splurgy as you want. Here is a realistic mid-range daily estimate per person, excluding flights and your hotel.

  • Food: 4,000-7,000 yen. Convenience-store breakfast, a ramen or set-menu lunch (~1,000 yen), and a sit-down dinner.
  • Transit: 800-1,500 yen with an IC card if you follow this grouped routing.
  • Attractions: 0-3,000 yen depending on how many observation decks and gardens you choose.
  • Buffer: Keep 2,000-3,000 yen for snacks, coffee, and souvenirs.

Call it roughly 8,000-14,000 yen a day for a comfortable mid-range trip. Bring some cash: many small izakaya, older shops, and shrine stalls are still cash-only, even as card and IC payments have spread.

Final Tips for a Smoother First Tokyo Trip

  • Avoid rush hour. Trains between roughly 7:30-9:00am and 5:30-7:00pm get genuinely packed. Time your longer hops around them.
  • Carry a small trash bag. Public bins are scarce, and eating while walking is frowned upon in many spots.
  • Learn three phrases. "Sumimasen" (excuse me), "arigatou gozaimasu" (thank you), and "kore o kudasai" (this one, please) go a long way.
  • Stand left on escalators in Tokyo, leaving the right side open for people walking up.
  • Reserve popular restaurants and Shibuya Sky in advance. Walk-ins work for most casual food, but the marquee spots fill up.

Four days is enough to fall for Tokyo without rushing, as long as you plan around neighborhoods instead of chasing a checklist of sights. Map your anchors, leave room to wander, and let the city surprise you in the gaps.
