Your First Solo Trip: A Guide from Preparation to Safety

2026-01-28 | myTravel Team

Setting off on a trip by yourself can feel intimidating, but it can also become one of the most meaningful and growth-inducing experiences of your life. Making every decision on your own, handling the unexpected alone, and meeting new people in unfamiliar places all build your confidence and independence in a big way. There's a reason so many seasoned travelers say that "deciding to travel solo was one of the best decisions I ever made."

Still, the first solo trip can feel daunting, and that's understandable. Will I be safe? Won't it feel awkward walking into a restaurant alone? What if I get lost? What if I feel lonely? Countless worries come first. This article was written to address those worries one by one. From choosing a destination to booking a place to stay, safety rules, communicating on the ground, and coping with loneliness, it covers everything you need for your first trip alone. By the time you finish reading this guide, your fear of solo travel may well turn into excitement.

Choosing a Destination: Countries Well Suited to a First Solo Trip

Half of whether your first solo trip goes smoothly is decided by your choice of destination. Pick a country that's too challenging for your first trip, and the journey itself can become a source of stress. There are four main things to consider when choosing a destination for a first solo trip: good public safety, well-developed public transport, a low language barrier, and solid travel infrastructure.

Japan is the country most often recommended for a first solo trip. It's one of the safest countries in the world, its public transport is punctual and convenient, and with convenience stores and restaurants everywhere, traveling alone is no trouble at all. In particular, dining alone is completely normal in Japan, so you can comfortably eat at a one-person ramen shop or a gyudon (beef bowl) place. Tokyo, Osaka, and Kyoto are great for a first visit, and traveling between cities by train is easy.

Taiwan is also very popular with solo travelers. It's safe, prices are affordable, and its night market culture means you can enjoy a wide variety of food even on your own. Locals are extremely friendly, and if you ask for directions they'll often go out of their way to help. Centered on Taipei, the MRT (subway) network is well developed, so getting around is easy too.

Singapore ranks among the safest places in the world, and since English is an official language, communication is no problem. As a city-state, distances are short, and with many cultures coexisting, you can have a rich experience even in a short stay. Thailand (Bangkok, Chiang Mai) is also known as a haven for solo travelers. Prices are low, and the infrastructure for solo travelers (hostels, day tours, and so on) is very well established. Around Khao San Road you can meet solo travelers from all over the world, so there's little chance of feeling lonely.

Top 5 recommended destinations for a first solo trip: Japan (Tokyo/Osaka), Taiwan (Taipei), Singapore, Thailand (Bangkok/Chiang Mai), Vietnam (Ho Chi Minh City/Hanoi). All are safe, reasonably priced, and have infrastructure that makes traveling alone easy.

Choosing Accommodation: Weigh Location, Reviews, and Safety Together

When traveling alone, you shouldn't choose your accommodation on price alone. You need to weigh all three of location, reviews, and safety to make for a comfortable and safe trip.

Location is the most important factor. Choose somewhere with easy access to public transport, with convenience stores or restaurants nearby, and close to a busy area that's safe to return to even late at night. No matter how cheap it is, it's best to avoid accommodation in isolated spots or areas with poor safety. It helps to check the location on Google Maps and preview the surroundings using Street View.

Be sure to read the reviews. In particular, if you look for reviews that include the keyword "solo travel," you can judge whether the place suits a solo traveler's perspective. Reviews can tell you whether the staff are friendly, what the security is like, and whether the facilities are clean. Choosing accommodation with a rating of 8.0 or higher (out of 10) is generally a safe bet.

As a type of accommodation for solo travel, we strongly recommend hostels. Contrary to the prejudice that "hostels are noisy and uncomfortable," recent hostels are clean and modern, with solid security systems in place. Above all, in a hostel's shared lounge, bar, and kitchen you can naturally connect with other travelers, which greatly reduces the loneliness of traveling alone. Many hostels also run women-only dormitories, so women traveling solo can use them with peace of mind.

Safety Rules: What Matters More When You're Alone

Safety is the biggest worry when traveling alone. But if you follow the basic safety rules, traveling solo can be plenty safe. Here are the core safety rules you should always follow when traveling alone.

First, share your itinerary with family or friends. Let at least one person know which city you're in, where you're staying, and roughly what your schedule looks like. If you build the habit of checking in once a day by messaging app or text, both sides can feel reassured. Sharing the itinerary you created in myTravel with your family lets them follow your route in real time, which is even safer.

Second, manage your valuables carefully. Keep your passport in the accommodation safe and carry a copy with you. Spread your cash across several places (wallet, money belt, room safe, and so on), and don't put everything in one place. Keep your phone and wallet in an inner pocket of your bag or in an anti-theft bag to prevent pickpocketing. Pickpocketing is especially common at tourist spots and on public transport in Europe, so extra caution is needed.

Essential safety rules: Don't walk alone down deserted alleys at night. Especially in a city you're visiting for the first time, stick to main roads and well-lit areas after dark. When taking a taxi, use an official taxi or an app-based ride service (Grab, Uber, and so on).

Third, minimize nighttime travel. Moving around alone late at night can be risky in any country. If you have to be out at night, stick to main streets and avoid deserted places. Arranging your evening plans near your accommodation to cut down on late returns is a good strategy too. In many cases, using a taxi app is safer than nighttime public transport.

Communication: Overcoming the Language Barrier

On a solo trip, communicating with locals can sometimes be a challenge, but at the same time it can be one of the most rewarding experiences. There are plenty of ways to communicate well even without perfect foreign-language skills.

Translation apps are an essential tool for the modern traveler. Apps like Google Translate, Papago, and DeepL handle text translation, and some can even translate signs or menus in real time when you point your camera at them. They're genuinely useful for reading a ramen shop menu in Japan or asking the price at a market in Thailand. If you download the offline translation feature in advance, you can use it even where there's no internet.

Learning a few basic greetings in the local language also helps a great deal. Being able to say just "hello," "thank you," and "how much is it?" in the local language completely changes how locals respond to you. A simple greeting in their language shows respect for the other person, and it often earns you warmer treatment in return. Knowing just "arigato gozaimasu" in Japanese, "khop khun khrap/kha" in Thai, or "xin chao" in Vietnamese makes travel much smoother.

Body language and a smile are also powerful communication tools. Even when words don't get through, a bright smile and a friendly gesture are a universal language. When asking for directions, show a map app and point to ask which way to go; at a restaurant, gesture toward another customer's dish and say "I'll have this," and most people will understand. The key to communication isn't perfect language but the will to communicate.

Coping with Loneliness: Traveling Alone but Not Lonely

One of the biggest worries about traveling alone is loneliness. Loneliness can wash over you especially when you're eating alone in the evening, or when you're taking in a beautiful view with no one to share it with. But there are more ways to cope with that loneliness than you might think.

Make use of the hostel community. As mentioned earlier, hostels are social spaces for solo travelers. Try being the first to strike up a conversation with another traveler in the shared lounge. Simple questions like "Where are you from?" or "Where did you go today?" get a conversation going. Many hostels host guest parties, cooking events, board-game nights, and the like, so join in actively.

Joining a day tour is another good approach. Taking part in locally run day tours, cooking classes, or walking tours lets you naturally mingle with other travelers. It's common for people on the same tour to have dinner together or team up on the next day's plans. If you look for tours marked "solo friendly" on booking sites, you can find programs that are easy to join on your own.

We also recommend spending time journaling or reading at a cafe. Rather than viewing time alone negatively, embrace it. Sitting at a cafe with a nice atmosphere, writing a travel journal and reflecting on your day, is a special luxury you can only enjoy when traveling solo. A travel journal becomes a precious memory later, and the very act of writing has the effect of sorting through your emotions and easing loneliness.

The Upsides of Solo Travel: This Is the Real Reason

The greatest gift solo travel gives you is complete freedom. You wake up when you want to in the morning, go where you want to go, eat what you want to eat, and rest when you want to rest. There's no need to sync schedules with anyone or to compromise. This freedom to change your plans on a whim depending on your mood that day is something you can only experience traveling alone.

Solo travel is also an opportunity for personal growth. Confidence grows from the process of deciding and handling everything yourself. Each time you find your way in an unfamiliar city, communicate with locals, and solve an unexpected problem, a sense of "I can do this" builds up. After you return from your trip, that confidence carries on in everyday life. Something people who've traveled solo commonly say is that "the world is kinder than I thought, and I'm stronger than I thought."

Finally, solo travel creates new connections. It may sound contradictory, but you actually end up meeting more people when you travel alone. When you have a group traveling with you, you tend to interact only within that group, but when you're on your own, you approach other travelers and locals with a more open mind. It's not rare for connections made this way to turn into lifelong friends.
