The Complete Visa Guide for International Travelers: Visa-Free, Visa-on-Arrival, and eVisas Explained

2026-07-10 | Hoonjae Park · myTravel Travel Content Team

Why Visa Rules Are the First Thing to Check

Booking a flight is the fun part. But the single most common reason travelers get turned away at the gate — before they even reach immigration — is a visa or passport-validity issue the airline catches at check-in. Airlines are legally responsible for carrying only passengers who can legally enter the destination, so they enforce these rules strictly.

Visa requirements depend on three things: your passport's country of issue, your destination, and the purpose and length of your stay. A U.S. passport holder and an Indian passport holder heading to the same country can face completely different rules. That's why generic advice from a friend is rarely enough — you need to verify for your own nationality.

There are broadly four scenarios you'll encounter: fully visa-free entry, an electronic travel authorization (like the U.S. ESTA), visa-on-arrival, and a traditional visa you must obtain in advance. The rest of this guide breaks down each one with concrete examples.

Plan the paperwork alongside the itinerary. When you build a trip in myTravel, keep your visa research notes and passport-validity deadlines in the same place as your day-by-day plan, so nothing critical falls through the cracks in a separate document you forget to open.

Visa-Free Entry: What It Really Means

Visa-free entry means you can enter a country for tourism (usually) without applying for anything in advance — you just show up with a valid passport. But "visa-free" almost never means "no rules." There are always conditions attached.

Common visa-free conditions

  • A maximum stay duration. Typical allowances are 30, 60, or 90 days. The Schengen Area, for example, allows many nationalities 90 days within any rolling 180-day period — and that window is cumulative across all Schengen countries, not per country.
  • Passport validity beyond your trip. Many countries require your passport to be valid for at least six months after your planned departure date. Some require only three months, and a few just want validity through your stay.
  • Proof of onward or return travel. Immigration officers and airline staff may ask to see a ticket out of the country.
  • Purpose limits. Visa-free usually covers tourism and sometimes short business meetings — but not paid work, long-term study, or journalism.

The Schengen 90/180 rule trips up a huge number of travelers. If you spend 90 days in Europe, you must then stay out of the entire zone for 90 days before returning. Track your days carefully if you're a long-stay traveler.

Electronic Travel Authorizations (ETAs)

An electronic travel authorization sits between visa-free and a full visa. You don't visit an embassy, but you do need to apply online — usually days before departure — and get approval linked to your passport. It's a lightweight screening step, not a stamp in your book.

The ones you're most likely to need

  • ESTA (United States) — for Visa Waiver Program nationals. Costs around $21, valid for two years or until your passport expires. Apply at least 72 hours before travel.
  • ETA (Canada) — roughly CAD 7, valid up to five years, tied to your passport.
  • ETA (United Kingdom) — rolled out broadly in 2025 for visa-exempt visitors; apply via the official app or website before you fly.
  • ETIAS (European Union) — the EU's upcoming pre-travel authorization for visa-free visitors to the Schengen Area. Check the official EU source for its current start date and fee before your trip.
  • K-ETA (South Korea) — required for many nationalities, though Korea has periodically waived it for certain countries, so verify the latest status.
Apply only through official government sites. Search results are full of third-party "visa services" that charge a large markup to submit the same free-or-cheap form. For ESTA, ETA, and ETIAS, go directly to the official .gov or europa.eu domain. If a site charges $80 for an ESTA, you're on the wrong one.

Visa-on-Arrival and eVisas

Some countries let you obtain a visa when you land, or apply for one entirely online before you leave. Both are convenient, but they have real trade-offs worth understanding.

Visa-on-arrival

You get the visa at the airport or land border. It's flexible, but you should be prepared for:

  • Cash payment in a specific currency (often U.S. dollars) — card machines may be down.
  • Passport-sized photos — bring two spares.
  • Queues that can eat an hour or more after a long flight.
  • The risk of refusal if you can't show a return ticket or accommodation.

eVisas

Countries like India, Vietnam, Egypt, Turkey, and Kenya offer eVisas you apply for online, typically 3–7 business days ahead. You upload a passport scan and photo, pay by card, and receive an approval PDF to print and carry. eVisas remove the airport uncertainty but require you to plan ahead — don't leave it to the night before your flight, because processing isn't always instant.

Whichever route applies, note the exact requirements and deadlines when you're organizing your trip. In myTravel, adding these details to your trip plan means they resurface as you review your itinerary in the days before departure — not buried in an email you have to search for.

How to Verify Requirements for Your Trip

Rules change frequently, and outdated blog posts (including this one, over time) can be dangerous. Here's a reliable verification process.

  1. Start with your own government's travel advisory site. Sites like the U.S. State Department, UK FCDO, or Canada's travel.gc.ca list entry requirements by destination for their citizens and are kept reasonably current.
  2. Cross-check the destination's official immigration or embassy site. This is the authoritative source. If the two disagree, the destination's own rules win.
  3. Use IATA's Travel Centre. The airline industry's Timatic database powers airline check-in decisions, so it reflects what the gate agent will actually see.
  4. Confirm passport validity math. If a country requires six months' validity from your entry date and your passport expires in five, you may be denied boarding — renew early.
  5. Check for transit visa needs. A long layover in a third country can require its own transit visa, even if you never leave the airport.
Build in buffer time. When you sketch out your trip dates in myTravel, count backward from your departure and set a personal deadline to finish all visa applications at least two weeks early. Embassy processing and passport renewals are the two things that most often derail a trip, and both reward starting early.

Common Mistakes That Get Travelers Denied

Most visa disasters come from a handful of avoidable errors. Watch for these:

  • Assuming a partner's or friend's rules apply to you. Requirements are per passport. Everyone in your group should verify individually.
  • Ignoring passport validity. This is the number-one cause of boarding denials. Check the six-month rule for every country on your route.
  • Overstaying a visa-free window. Overstays can trigger fines, deportation, and multi-year re-entry bans. Track the exact day limit.
  • Confusing single-entry and multiple-entry. If your visa is single-entry and you leave to visit a neighboring country, you may not be able to return on it.
  • Damaged passports. A torn page or water damage can lead to refusal. Inspect your passport well before you travel.
  • Leaving eVisa applications to the last minute. Processing delays do happen, and there's no fast-track once you're at the gate.

Getting your visa situation sorted is genuinely the least glamorous part of trip planning — but it's the part that determines whether the rest of your carefully built itinerary actually happens. Do the research once, note it clearly alongside your plans, and you can spend the rest of your prep time on the fun decisions.
