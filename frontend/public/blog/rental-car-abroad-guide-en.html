The Complete Guide to Renting a Car Abroad: From Booking to Insurance and Driving

2026-08-12 | Hoonjae Park · myTravel Travel Content Team

In regions where public transit is sparse, or on trips that hop freely between several cities, a rental car can be a huge help. You can go where you want, when you want, and when you have a lot of luggage or a larger group, it can even work out cheaper. But driving in an unfamiliar country also means there is a lot to keep track of, from road rules to insurance terms to the return procedure.

This article walks through it in order: when renting a car abroad makes sense, preparing an International Driving Permit, points to compare when booking, the insurance types that are easy to confuse, cautions for driving locally, and finally the return stage, where disputes happen most often. Even if this is your first time driving abroad, we will look at it from a practical angle so you can reduce mistakes. Detailed rules differ slightly by country and rental company, so treat what follows as a framework for grasping the big picture, and check it together with the actual terms of the company you book with.

1. When Renting a Car Abroad Makes Sense

A rental car is not a cure-all. On trips centered on large cities with good public transit, the stress of parking and the cost burden can actually be greater. So rather than defaulting to "a rental car no matter what," the right order is to first check whether this trip's travel pattern is a good match for a rental car. On the other hand, situations like the following are worth seriously considering a rental car for.

  • Areas with sparse public transit: Small towns and national parks in the U.S., Australia, and New Zealand, or rural villages in Europe, often have infrequent bus and train service, making a rental car essentially a necessity. Especially on itineraries touring vast natural landscapes, it is common to find places where stops are far apart or served only a few times a day.
  • Moving freely between multiple cities and bases: It is advantageous when you want to plan flexibly without being tied to fixed routes and timetables. The freedom to pull over and rest when you come across a view you like is another benefit unique to a rental car.
  • Lots of luggage, or a group of 3-4 or more: When split among the group, there are ranges where the per-person transport cost drops below public transit. If your trip involves large suitcases or outdoor gear, the ease of getting around also rises considerably.
  • Traveling with children or older adults: Door-to-door travel reduces physical strain, and unplanned stops for rest, restrooms, or snacks are easy to make.

Conversely, if your trip centers on cities with high downtown density and difficult parking, like Paris, Rome, or Tokyo, public transit may be more convenient and cheaper. Downtown, congestion, parking shortages, and expensive parking fees cancel out the advantages of a rental car. Remember that rental cars are strong for "travel between cities" and "exploring the outskirts" but weak "inside the city center," and the decision becomes easy.

For cost, too, do not look only at the "daily rental rate"; you should add up fuel, insurance, parking, and tolls and compare against the estimated cost of public transit. When these extra costs are added on top of a daily rate that looks cheap on the surface, public transit is often more economical on a short city trip.

Tip. A "mixed strategy"-public transit for city segments, a rental car only for the outskirts and nearby areas-is also a good choice. Renting the car only for the few days you need it, and returning it while you are in the city, can save you a great deal in parking fees and stress. For example, if you finish the city sightseeing first and then rent the car to line up with the day your countryside driving itinerary begins, you can cut down on unnecessary rental days.

2. Preparing an International Driving Permit (IDP)

In most countries, for a foreigner to drive you must carry both an International Driving Permit (IDP) and your home-country driver's license. Be sure to remember that an IDP is merely a document that translates and certifies your home license into several languages; it is not a license that lets you drive on its own. You must always carry your physical home license and passport together with it.

How to Issue One and the Conditions

  • Where to apply: You can apply and have one issued at Korea Road Traffic Authority driver's license test centers, police stations nationwide (civil affairs offices), and some international airports such as Incheon Airport. Airport issuance points can be used even on the day of departure, which is useful in a hurry, but it is safest to check the operating hours and location in advance.
  • Fee: 8,500 won.
  • Validity: One year from the date of issue. If your trip is longer than that, be mindful of the expiration date. If your trip extends beyond one year from issuance, you may be refused a rental locally, so it is better to adjust the timing of issuance to match your trip.
  • What to bring: Your own valid driver's license, passport, and one passport-style photo (this can vary by issuing office, so checking in advance is recommended).

Differences by Convention Type

International Driving Permits are divided into types depending on the convention they are based on, and the IDP recognized may differ depending on which convention the destination country has joined. Some countries additionally require an official translation of the home license or separate documents. Conversely, in certain countries an IDP alone is not enough, and if you stay beyond a set period you must convert to a local license, so for a long stay it is good to know this in advance too. Before departure, checking both the requirements of the destination country and the policy of the rental company you will actually rent from is the surest approach.

Also, an IDP is not a sticker affixed to your passport; it is issued as a separate booklet. Keep it well together with your passport and home license so you do not lose it during the trip, and it helps to save a copy or photo on your smartphone just in case.

Caution. An IDP is generally issued the same day you apply, but it can be rejected if the remaining validity of your passport or license is insufficient or the photo does not meet the specifications. Do not leave it until departure is imminent; get it issued with plenty of time to spare. Also, if there are several drivers, each person who will drive must be issued an IDP and registered as an additional driver at booking to be covered by insurance.

3. How to Book a Rental Car and What to Compare

You can rent a car broadly through a global rental company's official website, a price comparison / brokerage platform, or a small local operator. Comparison platforms are convenient because you can see the prices of several companies at a glance, but the final contract terms and insurance coverage must always be re-checked against the actual rental company's standards.

Items to Check Before Booking Without Fail

  • Is it the total or the base rate: Check whether the price shown on screen is the total including taxes, airport fees, and add-on services. It is common for only the "daily rate" to look low while extra charges are added on site.
  • Transmission type: The U.S. and Canada are mostly automatic (AT), but in Europe there are many manual (MT) cars and automatics tend to be pricier. If you are not comfortable driving a manual, clearly select "Automatic" when booking.
  • Fuel policy: Covered in detail later, but "Full-to-Full" (receive it full, return it full) is generally the most reasonable.
  • Mileage limit: Check whether it is unlimited mileage or has a daily/total km limit. For long-distance travel, whether it is unlimited matters.
  • One-way return fee: If you return the car somewhere other than where you rented it, a separate fee is often added.
  • Driver age and experience conditions: Those below a certain age (e.g., under 25) may face a "young driver" surcharge or restricted rental.
  • Cancellation and change policy: Check the conditions for free cancellation and the cutoff time. If your plans are fluid, it is more reassuring to choose free-cancellation, no-prepayment terms even if they cost a bit more.
  • Vehicle class (group): What is assigned at booking is usually not a "specific model" but a "vehicle group of the same class." Choose the class based on the trunk capacity and number of seats that suit your luggage and party, and in areas with many narrow alleys it is better to avoid an excessively large car.

Pickup / Return Location Strategy and Extra Charges

What most strongly influences the total in a booking is the pickup/return location and the add-on options. Deciding the following items in advance at the booking stage can reduce on-site surcharges.

  • One-way return fee: If you return the car in a different city or branch from where you rented it, a separate fee is often added. The fee tends to grow the more you cross borders or the greater the distance, so if your itinerary is pickup in City A and return in City B, be sure to check the one-way fee before booking. A round trip returning to the same branch can sometimes be cheaper.
  • Registering additional drivers: If several people take turns driving, each driver must be registered on the contract as an additional driver. If someone unregistered drives and causes an accident, the insurance can be voided. A daily rate per additional driver is often charged, so apply for it at booking.
  • Age limits and young-driver fees: Commonly, rentals are available from age 21, and those under 25 often face a "young driver" surcharge or restricted vehicle classes. Conversely, above a certain age rentals may also be restricted. The exact standards differ by company and country, so verification is needed.
  • Add-on equipment: Child seats, navigation, snow chains, and the like are usually paid options. Child seats are legally mandatory in many countries, so apply in advance if traveling with children.

Do Not Be Fooled by the Listed Price

The cheapest option on the comparison screen is not actually the cheapest. Behind a low base rate there may be hidden airport fees, additional driver fees, child seat / navigation rental fees, and insurance deductibles. On the screen just before final payment, be sure to re-check the "total" and "included / not included items," and capturing the terms on cancellation, fuel, and mileage gives you grounds if a dispute arises later.

Tip. The pickup location also affects the cost. Airport branches are convenient but often carry an airport fee (a premium-location charge), and a downtown branch is sometimes cheaper. If you do not plan to drive immediately after arriving, compare a downtown branch for pickup. Decide your payment card in advance too-rental companies mostly hold the deposit on the booker's own credit card, so a debit card or a card in someone else's name may be refused.

4. A Full Understanding of Insurance Types (CDW, LDW, SLI, PAI)

Insurance is the most confusing part of renting a car abroad, and at the same time the part where the cost difference in an accident is largest. It looks complicated because of all the abbreviations, but it is much easier to understand if you split it into insurance that covers "damage to my car" and insurance that covers "harm to the other party or occupants." The names and bundling differ slightly by company, so even the same abbreviation may cover a different scope; it is good to build the habit of checking based on "what, and up to how much, is covered" rather than on the abbreviation.

Abbr.NameCoverage
CDWCollision Damage WaiverWaives or reduces the driver's liability when the rented vehicle is damaged in an accident. However, a deductible (excess) often remains.
LDWLoss Damage WaiverA broader concept that adds theft on top of vehicle damage. Depending on the company, CDW and theft protection (TP) may be bundled and called LDW.
SLISupplemental Liability InsuranceInsurance that significantly raises the compensation limit for bodily and property harm caused to a third party (the other party) in an accident.
PAIPersonal Accident InsuranceCovers injury and medical costs for the driver and passengers themselves in an accident.

What Each Insurance Actually Covers and Excludes

Let's look more concretely at the "actual coverage" hidden behind the abbreviations. Even under the same name, detailed conditions differ by company, so treat the following as a general tendency.

  • CDW (Collision Damage Waiver): It reduces the driver's liability when the rented car is damaged in an accident, but it is less "insurance" and more a "waiver." That is, it is an agreement by the company not to bill you, and a deductible usually remains. Items easily excluded: tires, glass, the vehicle underbody, roof, and interior, along with towing and key loss, are commonly left out separately.
  • LDW (Loss Damage Waiver): A broader concept that adds theft protection on top of CDW. Depending on the company, CDW and theft insurance are bundled and called LDW, so check on the contract "whether theft is included."
  • SLI (Supplemental Liability Insurance): In regions where the base liability limit is set low (e.g., some U.S. states), it greatly raises the third-party compensation limit. Bodily-harm compensation for the other party can grow to a level that is hard to bear, so this limit is the key.
  • PAI (Personal Accident Insurance): Covers injury and medical costs for the driver and passengers themselves. If the travel insurance you already have covers injury and medical costs generously, this may overlap, so check your existing coverage first.

The Key Is the "Deductible (Excess)" and Super Cover

Even if you sign up for CDW/LDW, a deductible (excess) that the driver must bear up to a certain amount often remains in an accident. This amount can reach hundreds of dollars, so companies additionally sell a "Super Cover (Full / Zero Excess)" option to eliminate it. Super Cover often lowers the deductible to zero, or includes items that were originally excluded like tires and glass, but the scope differs by product, so you must check "what becomes zero." Since it is exactly this deductible that determines your burden in an actual accident, be sure to check the amount and the excluded items before signing up.

Using Your Credit Card's Rental Car Insurance

Some credit cards offer rental car damage insurance (of a CDW nature) as an add-on service. Used well, it can save you the counter insurance premium, but several conditions typically apply.

  • Payment condition: Coverage often requires that the full rental fee be paid with that card.
  • Pay-first, claim-later method: It is common that in an accident you pay first, then claim from the card issuer with documents (accident report, repair estimate, receipts). The processing flow differs from counter insurance, which waives liability immediately on site.
  • Liability not included: Card rental insurance usually covers only "damage to my car" and often does not cover third-party liability (bodily and property). This part must be arranged separately.
  • Vehicle / country / duration limits: Luxury cars, vans, and long-term rentals may be excluded, or coverage may be valid only in certain countries, so check the card issuer's terms before departure.

Another part easily missed is the items excluded from coverage. Tires, glass, the vehicle underbody, and roof, along with towing and key loss, are commonly left out of basic insurance. Off-road driving or crossing borders may be prohibited under the terms, and if you break this and have an accident, the insurance can be entirely voided. If such routes are in your plan, be sure to check whether they are allowed at the booking stage.

Caution. Coverage and processing differ depending on where you buy the insurance. Insurance bought at the rental company counter, separate excess (excess reimbursement) insurance sold on a booking platform, credit card add-on services, and travel insurance riders can get mixed up, leading you to over-insure or, conversely, leaving blind spots. Note too that credit card rental insurance is usually a "pay-first, then claim with documents" method and often does not cover liability (third-party compensation).
Tip. At the very least, never skip liability insurance (third-party compensation) for bodily and property harm. Your own car's repair cost will not exceed the value of the car, but if you cause serious bodily harm to another party, the compensation amount can grow to a level that is hard to bear. Even if you adjust the deductible level for damage to your own car according to your budget, when it comes to liability insurance it is safe to secure a generous limit.

5. Cautions for Driving Locally (Left-Hand Traffic, Fuel, Parking, Traffic Laws)

Once you have the car, now it is the actual road. Driving culture and rules differ by country, so learning the following items in advance before setting off will reduce moments of panic.

Left-Hand-Traffic Countries

The U.K., Japan, Australia, New Zealand, Thailand, and others are left-hand-traffic countries, where the driver's seat is on the right and vehicles run on the left side of the road. For drivers used to right-hand traffic, the following are especially confusing.

  • Because your lane sense is reversed, it is easy to unconsciously enter the opposite lane. It is especially easy to lose your bearings at intersections and roundabouts, so enter slowly.
  • Many cars have the turn-signal and wiper levers reversed, so beginners commonly turn on the wipers instead of the blinker.
  • With the driver's seat on the opposite side, your sense of the car's width changes, creating a tendency to drift toward the passenger side.
Tip. In a left-hand-traffic country, after picking up the rental car, try 5-10 minutes of practice driving in a parking lot or on a quiet road. Getting the roundabout entry direction into your body in particular is a great help.

Fuel

  • Check the fuel type: Confusing gasoline (petrol) with diesel when refueling can cause serious engine damage. Be sure to check the fuel type at pickup, and re-check the label inside the fuel filler flap.
  • Full-to-Full policy: Receiving it full and returning it full is generally the most reasonable. Fill up at a gas station near the return point before returning, and keep the receipt. In contrast, "receive full, return empty (Full-to-Empty)" prepays the fuel cost, so you can lose out on however much fuel is left.
  • Self-service refueling: The pay-before / pay-after method differs by country, and pump operation varies too. It can be confusing at first because procedures differ by region-inserting your card first and specifying an amount before refueling, or telling a pump number at the counter to pay. If it is your first time, use a station with an attendant, or watch how the person ahead of you does it and follow suit.

Parking

  • On-street parking is often divided by curb color and signs into whether it is allowed and during which hours. Parking without knowing the rules risks towing and fines. Look carefully too at day/time restrictions on signs and no-parking markings for cleaning days.
  • Paid parking is often settled at an unattended machine (parking meter) or by app, so check the payment method in advance. Some places take only coins or support only a local payment app, so you need to prepare so you are not caught off guard on arrival.
  • In areas where security is a concern, at night use an indoor parking garage or the lodging's own parking, and it is best not to leave valuables and luggage in the car. Items visible from outside the window become targets for break-ins.

Traffic Laws

  • Speed display unit: It is mostly km/h, but the U.S., U.K., and others use mph. Do not mistake the unit on the instrument panel.
  • Seatbelts in all seats and child car seats: Many countries make these mandatory, and when traveling with children it is good to apply for a child seat at the booking stage.
  • Drink-driving limits differ by country, and many are stricter than at home. If you have had anything to drink at all, do not drive.
  • Tolls and congestion charges: In areas with toll roads or downtown congestion charges, check in advance whether a transponder (electronic tag) is needed and how post-payment works. If you unknowingly pass a section where tolls are charged by unmanned camera, after the trip ends the toll may be billed together with a separate processing fee through the rental company.
  • Pedestrian and cyclist priority: In many countries pedestrian and cyclist protection is stronger than at home, such as always having to yield to pedestrians at crosswalks. Low-speed limits in school zones and residential areas must also be strictly observed.
  • Differences in signal and sign conventions: Whether right/left turns are allowed, priority for entering a roundabout, and rules for unprotected left turns differ by country. Do not assume it works the way you are used to; when in doubt, follow the flow of the car ahead and respond slowly.
Caution. There are many areas where signs and road guidance are written only in the local language. For navigation, prepare an offline map or an app with a stable data connection, and to prepare for mountain or outlying sections where the signal may drop, save your route in advance.

6. Real-World Scenarios by Situation

Applying theory to situations makes what you need to prepare even clearer. Using a few representative types of rental-car trips as examples, here is what is good to prepare in advance. Detailed rules differ by country and company, so refer to this as the big picture.

Preparing a U.S. West Coast Rental-Car Road Trip

The U.S. West Coast route linking national parks and small towns is a classic case where a rental car is essentially a necessity. Distances between cities are long, so first check whether it is unlimited mileage, and choose a vehicle class with room to spare for long distances. The speed unit is mph, and self-service refueling varies between pay-before and pay-after by branch, so it can be confusing at first. In vast stretches, gas stations are far apart, so a habit of filling up early when the fuel is about half is safe. If several people take turns driving, do not forget to register additional drivers, and if driving on unpaved forest roads inside national parks is in your plan, check whether it is allowed under the terms (a clause voiding insurance for off-road driving is common).

Cautions for First-Time Driving in Left-Hand-Traffic Japan

Japan is left-hand traffic, so the sense is reversed for drivers used to right-hand traffic. The driver's seat is on the right, and many cars have the turn-signal and wiper levers reversed, so at first it is easy to turn on the wipers instead of the blinker. Right after pickup, use 5-10 minutes of practice driving in a quiet spot to get the roundabout entry direction and the sense of the car's width into your body. If you will use toll expressways often, check whether an electronic toll-payment device (ETC card) is available for rental, and since downtown parking is often the unattended-settlement type, it is good to prepare your payment method in advance.

Europe's Small Towns: Tight Parking and Manual Transmission

Europe has many manual (MT) cars and automatics (AT) tend to be pricier, so if you are not comfortable with manual, you must clearly select "Automatic" when booking. Considering the narrow alleys and small parking spaces of old towns, it is better to avoid an excessively large car. On-street parking is split by curb color and signs into whether it is allowed and during which hours, and unattended machines or app payment are common. Some downtowns have congestion charges or low-emission zones (entry restrictions), and entering unknowingly can result in a fine afterward, so check the entry rules for your destination's downtown in advance.

Tip. Whatever the type, the common items to prepare are the same-your home license, IDP, and passport, the booker's own credit card, navigation that works offline (route saved in advance), and the photos and video to leave at pickup and return. Make sure not to miss these four in any country.

7. Return, Dispute Prevention, and Wrapping Up

The stage where disputes happen most often in a rental-car trip is, surprisingly, "the return." When a scratch supposedly discovered after return, cleaning fees, or a low-fuel charge are billed to your card belatedly, it is hard to respond. Just following these steps can prevent most disputes.

At Pickup - Leave Evidence

  • The moment you receive the car, meticulously record the entire exterior and existing scratches and scrapes with photos and video. Shoot the bumper, wheels, glass, and the interior, and it helps to have the shooting time recorded.
  • Photograph the fuel gauge reading and the odometer (instrument panel) too.
  • Check that existing damage is properly noted on the vehicle handover checklist (check-in sheet), and have any missing scratches marked and signed off on the spot.

At Return - Fix the Condition on Record

  • If you filled up per the fuel policy, keep the refueling receipt. For Full-to-Full, a receipt showing the location and time is good, so it is verifiable that you filled up at a gas station near the return point.
  • At return, photograph the exterior, fuel, and odometer again. Shooting from the same angles as at pickup makes comparison easy.
  • If possible, having staff verify the vehicle's condition with you and getting "no issues" in writing is safest. Unattended after-hours return (key drop) is convenient but vulnerable to later damage claims, so leave photo and video evidence especially meticulously.
  • The return time also matters. Going past your booked time can add extra charges, and since fees are calculated by the day, an overrun of a few hours can be billed a full day. For an airport return, factor in the time for refueling, returning the vehicle, and the shuttle, and arrive with plenty of time to spare.
Final Checklist Just Before Return
  • Did you fill up per the fuel policy and keep the refueling receipt (location and time)?
  • Did you re-photograph the exterior, interior, wheels, and glass from the same angles as at pickup?
  • Did you leave photos of the fuel gauge and odometer?
  • Did you leave no personal belongings in the car (charger, parking ticket, documents, etc.)?
  • Did you check whether there are items to be settled, such as tolls or parking violations?
  • Did you arrive with plenty of time so as not to exceed the booked return time?
Tip. The final bill is often finalized a few days after return. Compare the documents received at return against your card statement to check for any unexpected extra charges (cleaning fees, tolls, traffic-violation processing fees, etc.). If you have an objection, the photos and receipts you left at pickup and return become decisive evidence.

When You Have an Accident

Even for a light contact, do not settle on your own; follow the accident-reporting procedure the rental company provided. A report to the local police is often needed, and in some cases an accident report (police report) is required for the insurance to be processed. Be sure to secure the other vehicle's information (license plate, contact, insurance info), photos of the scene, and the accident location too.

Emergency situations like a breakdown or a flat can also be handled much more calmly if you prepare in advance. Save the roadside assistance contact you received at pickup, and check the location of the spare tire, warning triangle, and hazard lights. To prepare for a nighttime breakdown after hours, it is good to know whether 24-hour support is available.

Renting a car may seem like there is a lot to prepare, but in the end the core is three things: documents (license and IDP), insurance (especially third-party liability), and evidence (pickup and return records). Nail down just these three and you can drive with far more peace of mind even on unfamiliar roads. Organizing the pickup time, insurance options, return point, and required documents together in your itinerary reduces scrambling on site.
