10 Cultural Differences You Should Know Before Traveling in Europe

2026-02-16 | myTravel Team

Europe is one of the most popular overseas destinations for travelers from around the world. It's packed with world-famous landmarks like the Eiffel Tower in Paris, the Colosseum in Rome, and the Sagrada Familia in Barcelona, and each country holds its own distinct history and culture, so there's always something new to discover. But no matter how beautiful a destination is, not knowing the local customs can land you in awkward situations. Asking for water at a restaurant and getting handed a paid bottle of mineral water, showing up to shop on a Sunday only to find every store closed, or freezing up because you have no idea how much to tip — these are classic episodes of any European trip.

Knowing these cultural differences in advance not only helps you avoid those awkward moments on the ground, but also lets you connect more naturally with locals and experience the true charm of Europe. In this article, we'll take a detailed look at the 10 cultural differences travelers most often encounter in Europe. Each point includes specific country-by-country distinctions and practical tips, so it's well worth a read before you set off.

1. Meal Times and Dining Culture

Meal times in Europe differ greatly from what you may be used to. In many places lunch is around noon and dinner around 6–7 p.m., but southern European countries eat much later. In Spain, lunch runs from 2–3 p.m. and dinner doesn't start until 9–10 p.m. Show up at a restaurant at 8 p.m. and you'll find many places haven't even opened yet. Italy likewise starts dinner after 8 p.m., and in France 7:30–8 p.m. is typical.

By contrast, northern European countries like Germany, the UK, and the Netherlands eat dinner around 6–7 p.m. Remember that even within Europe, the north–south gap is significant.

The pace of a meal is also very different. In Europe, eating isn't simply about consuming food — it's a social occasion to enjoy over conversation. In Italy and France especially, a dinner that stretches over 2–3 hours or more is perfectly natural. The antipasto (starter), primo (first course), secondo (second course), dessert, and espresso all arrive slowly, course by course. Wolfing down your food and getting up to leave can actually come across as rude.

Also, at most European restaurants water costs money. Say "Water, please" and you'll be brought a paid bottle of mineral water (usually 3–5 euros). If you want free tap water, ask for "Tap water, please" in English, "Une carafe d'eau, s'il vous plait" in France, or "Acqua del rubinetto, per favore" in Italy. In Germany there's almost no custom of requesting tap water separately, so ordering water will nearly always get you a paid bottle.

Dining tip: If you want to eat dinner early in Spain or Italy, head for restaurants near tourist areas that cater to visitors rather than locals. These often open for dinner from around 6–7 p.m. But if you want to experience the real local hotspots, it's best to align with local meal times.

2. Tipping: Wildly Different by Country

Europe's tipping culture is different from that of the US, and it varies significantly from country to country. In Europe, a service charge is often already included in the bill, so there's no need to leave a 15–20% tip the way you would in the US.

In France, a service charge is legally included in the price (service compris), so tipping isn't mandatory. Still, if the service was good, it's polite to leave the coins from your change or add roughly 5–10% of the total. In Germany, it's common to round up the total. For example, if the bill comes to 18.50 euros, you'd hand over 20 euros and say "Stimmt so" (keep the change).

In Italy, a "coperto" (cover charge) of 1–3 euros is automatically added. Since this stands in for a service charge, an additional tip isn't required. In Spain, too, tipping isn't obligatory; if the meal was satisfying, leaving the change is plenty.

The UK has one of the more developed tipping cultures in Europe, with restaurants often adding a separate service charge of 10–12.5%. If a service charge is already included, no extra tip is needed, but if it isn't, leaving around 10% is customary.

In the Nordic countries (Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Finland), tipping culture is virtually nonexistent. Because service workers are paid well, tips aren't expected, and whether you leave one or not is entirely fine.

3. Greetings and Social Etiquette

Greetings in Europe differ from many other places and vary from country to country. Rather than bowing, a handshake or a cheek kiss (bise) is common in Europe.

In France, close acquaintances exchange light kisses on both cheeks (la bise). The number ranges from 2 to 4 depending on the region, and in Paris it's usually 2 (one on each cheek). For a first meeting, a handshake is enough. In the Netherlands people do three cheek kisses, while in Belgium it's just one.

In Germany and the UK, a handshake is the most common greeting, and cheek kisses are reserved for very close relationships. In Italy and Spain, kissing on both cheeks is widespread and happens naturally even between people of the same gender.

Greeting staff when you enter a shop or cafe matters, too. In France especially, saying "Bonjour" as you walk into a store is basic etiquette. Picking through the merchandise without a greeting can be seen as rude and may even change the quality of service you receive. On your way out, say "Au revoir" or "Merci."

4. Public Transport Etiquette

European public transport comes with a few etiquette rules that may be unfamiliar. First, the subways and trains in many European cities run on an honor system without automatic ticket gates. In Germany, Austria, and Switzerland especially, you board without any gate and inspectors check tickets at random. If you're caught without a valid ticket, you'll pay a fine of 30–60 euros, and "I'm a foreigner, I didn't know" won't get you off the hook.

Also, in many European cities you must validate your ticket after buying it. If you buy a train ticket in Italy or France, you need to insert it into the yellow validation machine at the station before boarding to stamp the date and time. An unvalidated ticket is treated as invalid and subject to a fine.

On subways and buses, avoid talking loudly on the phone. In Germany, Switzerland, Sweden, and other Nordic countries especially, quiet on public transport is highly valued. Playing music or videos out loud through a speaker without headphones is considered very rude in nearly every European country.

On escalators, the basic rule is to stand on one side and leave the other open. In the UK you stand on the right and walk on the left, but in most continental European countries standing on the right is the norm.

Transport tip: When traveling by train in Europe, some services require a seat reservation (high-speed trains like the TGV or AVE) while others let you board freely without one (Germany's RE/RB, Italy's Regionale, etc.). 5. Shopping Hours and Sunday Culture

In many places most stores stay open past 10 p.m. and 24-hour convenience stores are everywhere, but Europe is different. In many European countries shops close at 6–7 p.m., and most stores are shut on Sundays.

Germany is one of the strictest countries for Sunday trading. By law (Ladenschlussgesetz), almost all retail stores must close on Sundays. Supermarkets, clothing shops, electronics stores — all closed. The only places open tend to be shops inside train stations, gas-station convenience stores, and some bakeries. If you need something, be sure to buy it by Saturday.

In France, most shops are also closed on Sundays, and many supermarkets only open briefly on Sunday mornings. That said, in tourist hubs like the Marais district or the Champs-Élysées in Paris, some places do open on Sundays. In Spain, the siesta tradition means many shops still close between 2 and 5 p.m., a custom that remains especially strong in smaller towns.

By contrast, the UK is relatively free when it comes to Sunday shopping. Large supermarkets open for about six hours even on Sundays (typically 10 a.m.–4 p.m.), and small shops can trade without restriction. In the Netherlands, too, most stores in city centers open normally on Sundays.

6. Public Holidays and Festival-Season Cautions

Europe has more public holidays than many countries, and attitudes toward them are very different. In some places plenty of shops and restaurants stay open on holidays, but in Europe, when a holiday hits, everything really does stop. On December 25 (Christmas), January 1 (New Year), and Easter (a movable holiday in March–April) especially, even public transport may run a reduced service or shut down entirely.

During Italy's Ferragosto (around August 15) and France's August vacation season, small locally run restaurants and shops may close for 2–4 weeks. In Paris especially, some beloved eateries close for summer holidays in August, so if you're traveling then it's wise to check opening hours in advance.

On the flip side, making good use of festival season can lead to a special experience. Germany's Oktoberfest (September–October), Spain's Tomatina (August) and the San Fermín festival (July), Venice's Carnival in Italy (February–March), and the Netherlands' King's Day (April 27) are uniquely European events you can only experience at those times. Keep in mind, though, that accommodation prices can jump 2–3 times during festivals and booking gets hard, so it's best to reserve at least 2–3 months ahead.

7. Cash vs. Card: Differences in Payment Methods

Europe's payment culture varies enormously by country. Some countries are nearly fully cashless societies, while others still prefer cash.

The Nordic countries (Sweden, Denmark, Norway, Finland) have the most developed card payment culture in Europe. In Sweden, even street vendors and buskers accept card payments, and you can travel without using any cash at all. Some shops actually go cash-free and won't take cash.

Germany is, surprisingly, a cash-preferring country. Germans tend to favor cash for the sake of privacy and expense management. Large supermarkets and chain restaurants take cards, but at small eateries, bakeries, and traditional markets you may see a "Nur Barzahlung" (cash only) sign. When traveling in Germany, be sure to carry a reasonable amount of cash.

In Italy and Spain, too, small shops and bars sometimes only take cash. In smaller towns in southern Italy especially, card payment infrastructure can be lacking, so cash is necessary. France and the UK generally handle card payments well, but at traditional markets and small shops you may still need cash.

One more payment tip for traveling in Europe: when paying by card, you may be asked "In Euro or in your currency?" Always pay in the local currency (euros, pounds, etc.). Paying in your home currency triggers DCC (Dynamic Currency Conversion), an unfavorable exchange rate that adds a 3–5% surcharge.

8. Restroom Culture: Paid Toilets and How to Use Them

In many places public restrooms are free and kept clean, but in Europe a significant share of public toilets cost money. The fee for restrooms at train stations, tourist sites, and shopping malls is usually 0.50–1.50 euros, and coins are often required.

In Germany, train-station toilets are mostly paid (around 1 euro), and the Sanifair toilets at highway rest stops use a system where you insert 0.70 euros and get a 0.50-euro voucher back. In Italy, toilets near tourist sites are also mostly paid, and sometimes a cleaning attendant stands at the entrance to collect coins. In Paris, France has free self-cleaning street toilets (Sanisette) that you can make use of.

Tips for using free restrooms include going after you've ordered a meal or drink at a cafe or restaurant, or using the toilets in large department stores, shopping centers, or hotel lobbies. Fast-food outlets (McDonald's, Starbucks, etc.) are also convenient for restrooms, but some locations use a system where you have to enter a code printed on your receipt to unlock the door.

Restroom tip: When traveling in Europe, always carry a few 0.50-euro and 1-euro coins. Being caught without coins in an urgent moment can be a real problem. Also, make a habit of using the restroom before you leave your accommodation, and again when you dine at a restaurant or cafe — it can help cut down on paid-toilet costs.

9. Dress Codes at Tourist Sites

Europe's religious buildings (cathedrals, churches, mosques, etc.) have strict dress codes unlike many tourist sites elsewhere. At major cathedrals such as St. Peter's Basilica in the Vatican, Milan's Duomo, and Seville Cathedral, you can be turned away for wearing sleeveless tops, short shorts, miniskirts, or going barefoot.

Specifically, your shoulders and knees must be covered. Visit a cathedral in a sleeveless top and shorts on a hot summer day and you'll be sent away at the entrance. To be prepared, it's handy to always keep a thin scarf or cardigan in your bag. A single scarf can be draped over your shoulders or wrapped around your waist to cover your knees.

At some sites, wearing hats and sunglasses is also restricted. Taking off your hat inside a religious building is basic etiquette, and this rule is even stricter in Orthodox churches. Conversely, at Islamic mosques, women may be required to wear a headscarf in some places, so check the site's dress code before you visit.

Upscale and Michelin-starred restaurants may have dress codes as well. They typically require "smart casual" or above, and you can be turned away in sneakers, sandals, or sweatpants. If you've booked a Michelin restaurant in Europe, prepare a neat casual outfit or better.

10. Noise and Public-Space Etiquette

In Europe, and especially in northern and western Europe, people are very sensitive to noise in public spaces. Chatting animatedly with friends in a cafe or restaurant may feel natural elsewhere, but in Europe an excessively loud voice can draw disapproving looks from those around you.

In Germany, Switzerland, and Austria especially, noise regulations after 10 p.m. (Nachtruhe, quiet hours) exist by law. After this time, making loud noise in an apartment or hotel can prompt a neighbor to call the police. If you're staying in an Airbnb or apartment-style accommodation, be sure to keep this in mind.

Phone calls on public transport also require care. Taking a call on the subway may be common elsewhere, but in Europe talking loudly on the phone on public transport is considered very rude. Unless it's urgent, it's better to get off at the next stop to make your call. Watching videos or listening to music out loud through a speaker without headphones is a look-attracting behavior in nearly every European country.

When calling a server at a restaurant, you don't shout out loud the way you might elsewhere. In Europe you should make eye contact and raise your hand lightly, or quietly speak up as the server passes by. Calling out loudly or snapping your fingers is considered very rude. It's best to call politely in the local language: "Excusez-moi" (France), "Entschuldigung" (Germany), "Excuse me" (UK).

A Roundup of Country-Specific Quirks

Beyond the 10 points above, there are useful country-specific quirks worth knowing.

  • UK: Vehicles drive on the left. Automatically checking the right at a crosswalk can be dangerous. Crosswalks in London have "LOOK RIGHT" markings, so keep an eye out for them.
  • France: Greeting staff in French (Bonjour) in department stores and shops matters. Launching straight into English can earn you a cool reception.
  • Italy: Drinking standing at the counter (al banco) is cheaper than sitting down (al tavolo). At a famous cafe in Rome, an espresso is 1–1.50 euros standing, but can run 4–6 euros at a terrace table.
  • Switzerland: Prices are very high. A restaurant lunch commonly runs 25–40 Swiss francs, and even a bottle of mineral water is 3–4 francs. Buying groceries in advance at a supermarket (Migros, Coop) can cut costs dramatically.
  • Netherlands: The bicycle is the most important mode of transport. Standing or walking in a bike lane risks a collision, so walk clear of the red-marked bicycle-only lanes.
  • Spain: Due to the siesta culture, small shops close between 2 and 5 p.m. During these hours, rest at your hotel or relax at a cafe.

In Closing: Knowing the Culture Changes the Trip

In European travel, there's a world of difference between going in knowing the cultural differences and going in without a clue. Meal-time differences, tipping, greetings, public-transport etiquette, shopping hours, holidays, payment methods, restroom culture, dress codes, and noise etiquette — remembering just these 10 things will greatly reduce the chances of being caught off guard in Europe.

Respecting cultural differences and making the effort to adapt to local ways leaves a good impression on the people you meet and leads to a richer travel experience. Growing from a "tourist" into a "traveler" — that is the power of travel grounded in understanding culture.
