How to Survive a Long-Haul Flight: From Seat Selection to Sleep

2026-07-10 | Hoonjae Park · myTravel Travel Content Team

Why Long-Haul Flights Wear You Down

A 12-hour flight is not just a longer version of a 2-hour hop. Your body sits in one position, the cabin air runs dry, the pressure sits at the equivalent of roughly 6,000-8,000 feet of altitude, and your circadian rhythm gets dragged across five or ten time zones. The result is the familiar cocktail of a stiff back, swollen ankles, a pounding headache, and jet lag that lingers for days after you land.

The good news is that most of this discomfort is preventable. The difference between arriving wrecked and arriving functional usually comes down to a handful of choices you make before you even board: which seat you booked, what you packed in your personal item, and how you plan to eat, drink, and sleep on the plane.

This guide walks through those choices in order, from the moment you pick a seat to the moment you finally drift off somewhere over the ocean. None of it requires a business-class ticket. It just requires a little planning.

Choosing the Right Seat

Seat choice has the single biggest impact on your comfort, and it costs nothing extra if you plan ahead. The classic debate is window versus aisle, and the right answer depends on what you value most.

  • Window seat: Best if sleep is your priority. You get a wall to lean against, control over the shade, and nobody climbing over you. The trade-off is that you have to disturb your neighbors to reach the bathroom.
  • Aisle seat: Best if you plan to get up often, have a smaller bladder, or simply hate feeling boxed in. You can stretch one leg into the aisle and stand up whenever you like. The downside is the elbow and cart traffic.
  • Middle seat: Avoid it on long-haul flights whenever you possibly can.

The bulkhead and exit row question

Exit rows and bulkhead seats offer more legroom, but they come with catches. Exit-row seats often have fixed armrests and reduced recline, and you take on responsibility for operating the door in an emergency. Bulkhead seats give you a wall in front of you instead of a reclining seatback, which means no under-seat storage during takeoff and landing.

Seats to actively avoid

  • The very last row, which usually does not recline and sits next to the noisy, smelly galley and lavatories.
  • Rows directly in front of an exit, which sometimes have limited recline for safety reasons.
  • Anything right beside a bathroom, where you get a steady stream of foot traffic and odors all night.

A quick check of a seat-map site before you book will flag most of these problem seats. Spending five minutes here saves you twelve hours of regret.

Dress for the Cabin, Not the Airport

What you wear onboard matters far more than what looks good in the departure terminal. Cabins run cold at cruising altitude, often around 22-24°C but with strong drafts, and your body temperature drops when you sleep. The goal is loose, breathable layers you can add or remove.

  • Loose, soft layers: A t-shirt, a light hoodie or cardigan, and a warm scarf that doubles as a blanket or pillow. Skip tight jeans and anything with a stiff waistband that digs in when you sit for hours.
  • Compression socks: This is the single most underrated item for a long flight. Graduated compression socks help prevent the ankle and calf swelling that comes from sitting still, and they lower the risk of blood clots on flights over four hours. Put them on before you board.
  • Slip-on shoes: Your feet swell in flight, so wear shoes you can loosen or remove easily. Bring clean socks to change into so you are not padding to the bathroom barefoot.

Hydration and What to Eat

Cabin humidity often sits below 20 percent, drier than most deserts. That dryness is behind a surprising share of in-flight misery: headaches, scratchy throat, dry eyes, and fatigue that feels worse than the time change alone would explain.

Drink more water than feels necessary

A useful rule of thumb is roughly 250 ml of water for every hour in the air. Bring an empty reusable bottle through security and fill it at a fountain before boarding, so you are not waiting on the drinks cart every time you get thirsty. Ask the crew to refill it during the flight.

Go easy on alcohol and caffeine

Both are diuretics, and both hit harder at altitude. A glass of wine at 35,000 feet dehydrates you faster and disrupts the quality of any sleep you manage to get. If you want to sleep on the flight, a single drink is a poor trade. Caffeine is fine early on but cut it off several hours before you plan to rest.

Eat lighter than usual

  • Heavy, salty airline meals cause bloating and water retention, which worsens the swelling in your legs.
  • Consider pre-ordering a lighter special meal, such as a fruit plate or a vegetarian option, when the airline allows it.
  • Pack your own snacks like nuts, fruit, or a sandwich so you are not stuck eating whatever arrives at an inconvenient hour.

Actually Sleeping in Economy

Real sleep in an upright economy seat is hard, but it is far from impossible. The trick is to recreate as much of your normal sleep environment as you can and to time it around your destination.

Set your watch to your destination early

The moment you board, mentally switch to your arrival time zone. If it is nighttime where you are landing, try to sleep even if your body says otherwise. If it is daytime there, stay awake and let yourself fully crash when you arrive. This small mental shift is one of the most effective jet-lag defenses there is.

Build a sleep cave

  • Eye mask: A cabin never gets fully dark, and neighbors leave reading lights on. A good contoured eye mask blocks it out.
  • Noise-cancelling headphones or foam earplugs: Engine drone, crying children, and cart rattle all vanish. This is worth the investment more than almost any other travel gadget.
  • Neck pillow: A proper one keeps your head from lolling forward and waking you. Inflatable versions pack down small.
  • Recline early: Recline your seat right after the meal service ends, before the person behind you settles in, and be gracious about it.

Move your body on a schedule

Every couple of hours, get up and walk the aisle for a few minutes, and do simple seated exercises in between: ankle circles, calf raises against the floor, and shoulder rolls. This is not just about comfort. Movement is your main defense against deep vein thrombosis on long flights, and it makes the swelling in your legs far more bearable.

Beating Jet Lag After You Land

The flight is only half the battle. What you do in the first 24 hours on the ground decides how quickly your body adjusts to the new time zone.

  • Chase daylight: Sunlight is the strongest signal for resetting your internal clock. If you land in the morning, get outside and stay active. If you land at night, keep the lights low and wind down.
  • Resist the early nap: A short 20-minute power nap is fine, but crawling into bed at 3 p.m. local time will lock in your jet lag for days. Push through to a normal local bedtime.
  • Eat on the local schedule: Meal timing helps anchor your body clock. Even if you are not hungry, eating dinner at the local dinner hour speeds the adjustment.
  • Keep the first day light: Don't pack a marathon of sightseeing into your arrival day. Ease in, then hit the ground running once you've had one solid night's sleep.

Long-haul flying will never be luxurious in the back of the plane, but it does not have to leave you broken. Pick a smart seat, dress in layers, drink far more water than you think you need, guard your sleep like it matters, and treat that first day on the ground with a little patience. Do that, and you'll spend your trip exploring instead of recovering.
